# HOW TO READ THIS DOCUMENT

This is a research compilation, not a storybook — but it is written to be read. A few conventions will help you navigate it.

**Dates.** "ka" means *thousand years ago*; "Ma" means *million years ago*. So 26.5 ka = 26,500 years ago. Where calendars matter I use BC/BCE. The Last Glacial Maximum, the deepest freeze of the last ice age, ran from roughly 26,500 to 19,000 years ago. I will call it **the LGM** throughout.

**Pedigree tags.** Every factual claim carries a tag showing how well it is established, because in this field the difference between "proven," "probable," and "hotly disputed" is the whole story:

- **[S]** — Strong: peer-reviewed, replicated or consensus-grade (major journals: *Nature*, *Science*, *Cell*, *PNAS*, etc.)
- **[P]** — Preprint: credible, influential, but not yet through peer review
- **[C]** — Contested: serious scientists disagree; both sides are presented
- **[M]** — Media or single-source: interesting, unverified; never load-bearing

**Why tags matter here.** Ice-age science moves fast and fights hard. In the last five years alone, the field has produced a fake-catastrophe scandal (Toba), a dating-method war (uranium-thorium cave art), two rival human-bottleneck papers that cannot both be right, and a redating attack on the most famous early site in the Americas (Monte Verde). A reader who knows *how much* confidence each claim deserves is a reader who cannot be misled. That is the standard this document applies to itself.

**A note for the non-specialist.** This document was built to be read by anyone willing to read it properly. If a sentence stacks unfamiliar terms, slow down: nearly every technical word is defined in plain language where it first appears, and Appendix B collects them all. The bracketed tags — [S], [P], [C], [M] — tell you at a glance whether a claim is solid, fresh, disputed, or merely reported; you can skim them on a first reading and lean on them on a second. The tables (the isotope-stage ledger, the master timeline, the controversy index) are reference furniture: consult them when the text points, don't memorize them. And if one part is denser than the rest — Part Eight, the genetics — it says so at its own threshold. Read in order the first time; dart thereafter. Nothing in these pages requires a science degree. It requires only what the ice age itself demanded: patience, and attention to the connections.

**The cast.** Three kinds of humans walked through the ice age: our own species, *Homo sapiens*; the Neanderthals; and the Denisovans — plus at least two island relict species, the "hobbits" of Flores and the mysterious *Homo luzonensis* of the Philippines. All of them were, at one point or another, refugees from the ice.

# PROLOGUE: THE SEA CAME BACK

Between 26,500 and 19,000 years ago, so much of Earth's water was locked in ice sheets up to three and a half kilometers thick that the ocean fell between 120 and 135 meters below its present level [S — EPILOG synthesis, *Quaternary Science Reviews* 2002; Lambeck et al., *PNAS* 2014]. That is not a small number. A 130-meter drop exposes the entire Sunda Shelf — an area roughly the size of Europe — joining Borneo, Java, Sumatra, and the Malay Peninsula into one continuous landmass called **Sundaland**. It turns the Bering Strait into a thousand-kilometer-wide grassland, **Beringia**, connecting Siberia to Alaska. It pushes the coastline of every continent outward by tens to hundreds of kilometers.

People lived on all of it.

Then the ice began to melt, and the sea came back — at times faster than a meter per decade [S — Deschamps et al., *Science* 2012, for the pulse of 14,600 years ago]. The refugees of the ice age were not only people fleeing cold. They were also people fleeing *water*: the returning ocean that erased homelands, drowned hunting grounds, broke land bridges, and turned coastal plains into archipelagos within a few human lifetimes. The Aborigines of Australia preserved accurate memories of those drowned coasts in oral tradition for ten thousand years [S — Nunn & Reid, *Scientific American* 2015; *Quaternary Science Reviews* 2016]. The Mesopotamians wrote their version into the Epic of Gilgamesh, and from there it passed into Genesis. The drowned world is not a metaphor. It is the bottom of the sea, and it is full of human sites.

This document is the fullest available account of that double exile — cold first, then water — and of what it made of us. It begins before the beginning, with the ice ages that made our species, and it ends today, with the genes, the languages, the diseases, the myths, and the ethical arguments that the ice age left behind.

# PART ONE — THE MACHINE: HOW EARTH'S ICE AGES WORK

## 1.1 The Quaternary: a planet with a heartbeat of ice

We live in an interglacial — a warm pause inside an ice age that has been running for about 2.6 million years. The **Quaternary** period, which began 2.588 million years ago (its formal start marked by the marine isotope stage MIS 103), is defined by its rhythm: more than fifty large swings between cold glacials and warm interglacials. For its first two million years the swings came roughly every 41,000 years, matching the tilt of Earth's axis; then, about a million years ago, the pattern lengthened into the deep 100,000-year cycles that have ruled ever since — a transition called the **Mid-Pleistocene Transition**, which matters enormously to our story because the most violent scientific dispute in human-origins genetics (Part Two) is anchored to it.

This was not Earth's first ice age. The **Permo-Carboniferous** glaciation ended around 250 million years ago, long before any hominin existed; the Pleistocene is simply the most recent chapter of a planet that periodically locks a third of its water into ice. What makes the Quaternary *our* ice age is that every member of the human lineage since *Homo habilis* lived inside it. We are, structurally, ice-age animals having a warm spell.

The pacemaker is celestial. Earth's orientation and orbit wobble in three overlapping cycles — **precession** (the ~21,700-year wobble of the spin axis, which changes which hemisphere faces the sun at closest approach), **obliquity** (the ~41,000-year tilt of the axis, which varies how extreme the seasons get), and **eccentricity** (the ~95,800-year stretch of the orbit itself, which modulates how much precession matters). None of them changes how much sunlight Earth receives overall; they redistribute it in space and time, tipping the balance between ice accumulation and ice melt. Their dominance has shifted through the Quaternary: precession ran the early ice ages, obliquity dominated from three million to one million years ago, and eccentricity — the 100,000-year heartbeat — has paced the last million years [S — Richerson et al., Pleistocene culture synthesis, UC Davis].

Here is the counterintuitive fact on which this entire document rests: **ice ages lower the sea and open the land; warm periods raise the sea and drown it.** At a glacial maximum the ocean falls more than 120 meters, exposing the Sunda Shelf (an area the size of Europe), the Bering land bridge (a thousand kilometers wide), the Persian Gulf basin (the size of Great Britain), the floor of the North Sea, and every coastal plain on Earth. At the warm peak, all of it goes back under. Migration routes, refuges, and homelands have been swapped on this cycle for as long as our genus has existed. Being a refugee is not an accident of human history; it is a recurring feature of the human condition, written into the planet's operating system.

Culture tracks this table with eerie fidelity: the Middle Paleolithic begins in MIS 6, modern humans evolve; the first sapiens leave Africa in MIS 5; the Upper Paleolithic, cave art, and the peopling of Australia all belong to MIS 3; the LGM (MIS 2) squeezes humanity into the refuges this document maps.

## 1.3 Ice as evolutionary engine

Climate did not merely harass our ancestors; it built them. Three influential frameworks describe the mechanism [S — Potts, *Quaternary Science Reviews* 2021; Springer 2009], and a fourth line of evidence tests them:

- **Variability selection** (Rick Potts, Smithsonian): unstable climates don't just select for strength or cold tolerance — they select for *generality*. Species that can move, adapt behaviorally, and think their way through anything outlast specialists. On this view, big brains, complex sociality, technological flexibility, and long childhoods are the entry fees the ice ages charged for admission.
- **Turnover pulses** (Elisabeth Vrba): abrupt, *directional* climate shifts drive waves of extinction and speciation — faunas turn over wholesale, and the hominin fossil record shows several such pulses matching climatic transitions.
- **Pulsed climate variability** (Mark Maslin and Martin Trauth): eccentricity-paced "packets" of extreme climate variability — arriving roughly every 400,000 years, and dated to about 2.6, 1.8, and 1.0 million years ago — line up with the major transitions of hominin evolution, including the emergence of the genus *Homo*, the first stone-tool industries, and the earliest dispersals out of Africa.

The geological record is on their side. In Kenya's Turkana Basin, leaf-wax hydrogen isotopes document millennial-scale climate swings 1.72 to 1.6 million years ago, with arid-to-humid shifts in **under two centuries** — a pace no slow adaptation could track, and exactly the kind of variability that pays generalists [S — *Quaternary Science Reviews* 2021]. In China, the density of hominin archaeological sites tracks the glacial-interglacial rhythm across dozens of marine isotope stages, peaking in the warm packets and thinning in the cold [A — Land, MDPI 2023]. The archaeological census agrees across the whole span: hominin site density rises and falls with the ice for 2.6 million years — a dress rehearsal, at the scale of an entire genus, for the single ice age this document will trace in detail.

The ice was our schoolmaster long before it was our enemy. And like every schoolmaster, it kept attendance: the cold episodes are precisely where the fossil and archaeological record goes thin, because the populations that weathered them were small, cornered, and concentrated in the refuges this document maps.
## 1.4 Where the word "refuge" comes from

The **refugium** concept was born not in human prehistory but in botany and zoology, and its history is a warning dressed as a definition. Watching how species survived glaciations by contracting into small pockets — the Iberian, Italian, and Balkan peninsulas for European trees and animals — biologists formalized the idea through the young field of **phylogeography** (John Avise coined the term in 1987; Geoffrey Hewitt of the University of East Anglia built the European refugial framework across the 1990s) [S — Stewart, Lister, Barnes & Dalén, *Proceedings of the Royal Society B* 2010].

The formal definition: a refugium is a region occupied by a species during the period of *maximum range contraction* in a glacial-interglacial cycle. Hewitt's three classic models describe how Europe was repopulated afterward — a single Balkan source; three peninsular sources; or a dual Iberian-plus-Balkan source — and each predicted different genetic signatures in modern animals, which is what made the idea testable. The discovery of **cryptic northern refugia** then overturned the simple picture: small mammals, land snails, freshwater fish, ferns, and sedges survived *north* of the conventional ice limits, in periglacial pockets and sheltered valleys — Stewart and Lister's 2001 paper founded that line, Provan and Bennett's 2008 review consolidated it, and Hampe and Petit's "rear edge" concept explained why the *southernmost* populations of a species are often not its refuge but its relict. Plant macrofossils pushed furthest: one study dated a hundred and forty charcoals to show beech and Scots pine persisting in Loire and Garonne valley microrefugia straight through the coldest Heinrich Stadials; Scandinavian sedges carry genetic lineages that survived on-site through the Weichselian ice age [S — de Lafontaine 2014; Bruederle 2019 genomics]. The skeptic's counter-review — Tzedakis's "Cryptic or mystic?", cited over 440 times — keeps the method honest by showing how easily wishful refugia are inferred from ambiguous data.

Why does any of this matter for human beings? Because the natural temptation is to treat *Homo sapiens* like any other mammal — and it fails. A 2024 review of European mammal genetics found clean refugial signatures in brown bears (a Spanish haplotype living in LGM Belgium), red deer and water voles (identical populations on both sides of the freeze), and bank voles (cryptic northern refugia) — and *no comparable pattern in humans*: our only refugial trace is slightly elevated private allele diversity in western Europe during the Mesolithic, the fingerprint of post-LGM re-expansion and mixing rather than peninsular isolation [S — *Diversity* (MDPI) 2024, 16:611]. Humans, uniquely among mammals, rebuilt networks *during* the coldest millennia (Part Four, the Peña Capón exchange). Our refugia were real — but they were connected.

And a cautionary tale hangs over the whole enterprise. The **Amazon refugia hypothesis** — Jürgen Haffer's 1969 proposal that Pleistocene climate cycles fragmented the Amazon forest into isolated pockets where birds (and then plants, lizards, and, in Betty Meggers's extension, *languages*) diverged — was extended from birds to plants to linguistics with increasing confidence, and then largely collapsed when pollen records showed the Amazon forest persisting through the LGM [S — "What has become of the refugia hypothesis," PMC6468052]. Refugium logic, pushed one analogy too far, breaks. This document will try never to push it that far — and will show its work every time.

## 2.1 The war of the bottlenecks

Did humanity nearly die before it began? In 2023, a team led by Haipeng Li at the Shanghai Institute of Nutrition and Health published a claim in *Science* that electrified and divided the field [S for the paper's existence; C for its verdict — Hu et al., *Science* 381:979–984].

Using a new coalescent method called **FitCoal** (fast infinitesimal time coalescent), applied to the site frequency spectrum of 3,154 present-day genomes from ten African populations, they proposed that between **930,000 and 813,000 years ago** — a span of roughly 117,000 years — our ancestral population collapsed to about **1,280 breeding individuals** (the paper's body text gives 2,808, an internal discrepancy worth noting), losing about 98.7% of its numbers at the onset. They linked the collapse to the Mid-Pleistocene Transition, when glacial cycles lengthened, and to glacial MIS 22 — which happens to be when continental ice sheets first spread extensively across North America and Eurasia, around 850,000 years ago. They further attributed 65.85% of our present genetic diversity to losses suffered in this event, and argued that the fusion of human chromosome 2 may date to it, with the sapiens/Neanderthal/Denisovan split coming *after* 813,000 years ago. The bottleneck was weak or undetectable in forty non-African populations, which they attributed to the later out-of-Africa bottleneck masking it.

Then the counterattack. In 2024, Manoharan, Cousins, and colleagues posted a demolition job [published after peer review; C]: FitCoal's bottleneck model, they showed, fits the genomic data *worse* than a plain no-bottleneck model — by likelihood differences of 69,903 points on one dataset and 1,084 on another — and a bottleneck that severe should have left detectable scars in out-of-Africa populations where FitCoal finds none. The deepest problem is structural: the method assumes a single randomly mating (panmictic) population, and humans demonstrably were not one. Cousins's own work (below) shows our ancestry descends from at least two deeply divergent populations; and in structured populations, coalescence rates simply do not equal population size — long average coalescence times can be produced by structure alone, a result going back to Mazet and colleagues in 2016. Deng, Nielsen, and Song (*Genetics* 2024) called the bottleneck "likely a statistical artifact"; Terhorst failed to replicate it independently.

There is one independent line of support: Michele Muttoni and colleagues argued in *PNAS* (2024) from paleomagnetic, fossil, and climatic evidence that a hominin population bottleneck did coincide with late Early Pleistocene glacial maxima and migration barriers, and that it plausibly drove the transition from *Homo erectus* to a Middle Pleistocene lineage [S — *PNAS*, cited 25 times]. Chris Stringer of the Natural History Museum, London, remains skeptical in a precise way: hominin fossils — antecessor, erectus, heidelbergensis candidates — exist *inside* the proposed window, and a truly global near-extinction should have left a universal fossil gap. The effect, if real, was probably regional, not planetary.

**Current verdict [C]:** the severe 930 ka bottleneck is unproven and possibly an artifact of violated assumptions; the deep-structure models below fit the same data better and make the same predictions more parsimoniously. Note the elegant convergence of the two fights: the FitCoal team's catastrophe and the structure model's long isolation generate *the same genomic signature*, and both cannot be the whole truth. Both pedigrees are retained throughout this document, because the writing rule here is that contested claims travel with their contest.

## 2.2 The weakly structured stem: a braided river, not a tree

The most important idea in recent human-origins science is that our African ancestry was not a single breeding population but a **braided river** of intermingling lineages — several streams, separating and rejoining over a million years, only one of which carries your ancestors. Two landmark models define the current state:

**The weakly structured stem** — Ragsdale et al., *Nature* 2023 [S — PMC10208968]. Present-day African population structure, on this account, dates only to MIS 5 — the earliest divergence among living populations is the Khoe-San versus everyone else, 120,000–135,000 years ago. Before that, the model posits two or more "stems" — weakly differentiated populations exchanging genes continuously. Under the continuous-migration version, the stems split 1.22 million years ago and never fully stopped exchanging migrants (about 1,089 thousand years of gene flow); under the merger version, they split 1.69 million years ago and mostly stayed apart. The southern stem fractured around 478,000 years ago (95% confidence 276–478 ka) into a southern branch — ancestral to the Khoe-San — and an eastern branch ancestral to everyone else. The mergers came late and fast: the Nama descend from about 30% of the southern stem and 70% of the other, merging ~119,000 years ago (101–125 ka confidence interval); most other Africans merged roughly 50/50 around 100,000 years ago. Strikingly, the Mende of West Africa received an additional pulse of about 19% replacement ancestry from one stem only **25,000 years ago — squarely in the LGM** (22–26 ka confidence interval). The model needs no ghost archaic populations at all: structure alone explains the signals others attributed to extinct interbreeding species.

**The cobraa two-population model** — Cousins, Scally, and Durbin, *Nature Genetics* 2025 [S]. Their hidden-Markov analysis of whole genomes finds two ancestral populations that diverged around **1.5 million years ago**, remained isolated for over a million years, then admixed about **300,000 years ago in an 80:20 ratio**. Three details matter. First, the minority-population ancestry is concentrated *away* from gene-coding sequence — a classic signature of mildly deleterious ancestry being purged once it lands on a new genetic background. Second, the majority population passed through a strong bottleneck immediately after the 1.5-million-year split — precisely the kind of event that can masquerade, downstream, as a near-extinction to methods that assume panmixia. Third, the majority-population ancestry correlates with the regions where humans and Neanderthals/Denisovans diverge — meaning the structured stem likely also fed the archaic lineages; the split between "us" and "them" is younger than the split between the stems.

Notice the elegance: the FitCoal team's catastrophe at 930 ka and the cobraa team's million-year two-population history may be **two readings of the same genomic fact**. One says "humanity nearly died 900,000 years ago." The other says "humanity was two lineages for a million years and merged 300,000 years ago; the merger's echo looks, to a panmictic method, like a death." Both cannot be the whole truth — and the next section shows that even the ghosts are being pulled into the same structure.

## 2.3 The ghost in everyone's genome

Beyond the named archaics, genomics now insists on at least two **ghost** populations — lineages inferred from their genetic shadow, never excavated. The capstone arrived in July 2026, when the Berkeley lab of Priya Moorjani published what may be the deepest such study yet [S — Zhang, Biddanda, Johnson, O'Dushlaine & Moorjani, *Science*, doi 10.1126/science.aef8874; earlier preprint of March 2026].

Their method, TRACE, runs on **ancestral recombination graphs** — complete maps of how every stretch of every genome coalesces backward in time, built by the SINGER algorithm — and scans them for segments that coalesce deeper than the Neanderthal and Denisovan splits (a cutoff of 15,000 generations, about 420,000 years). The safeguards are severe: false-discovery rates below a quarter of one percent, recall near 80 percent on simulated data. The results:

- **A ghost archaic lineage in every sampled population** — British, Chinese, Indian, Yoruba, Luhya alike — totaling 0.49 to 1.1 percent of genomes. The ghost coalesces around **830,000 years ago** (95% confidence 0.61–1.27 million years), and it is *roughly equally related* to Neanderthal and Denisovan genomes — a sister of both that has never been found as a fossil.
- Most of the ghost ancestry in non-Africans is shared with sub-Saharan Africans, which places its admixture **before the out-of-Africa exit** — the same placement the contested West African models below arrived at by a different road.
- The ghost persists **inside** the so-called "archaic deserts" — genomic regions previously thought completely free of archaic ancestry. The deserts, it turns out, rejected *specific* archaic lineages (Neanderthal and Denisovan ancestry is purged there), not archaic ancestry as such. Selection, in other words, was lineage-specific, not blanket.
- In Oceanian genomes (92 individuals from Papua New Guinea, Vanuatu, and the Santa Cruz Islands), they separately measured 0.73 percent Neanderthal, 0.66 percent Denisovan, and 0.33 percent ghost — plus **super-archaic ancestry flowing through Denisovans**, an *erectus*-grade lineage diverging perhaps 1.8 million years ago, reaching modern humans via Denisovan intermediaries. The total archaic fraction of a modern human genome, the authors note, is about 2 percent.

The older ghost claim belongs to Durvasula and Sankararaman (*Science Advances* 2020, cited 207 times) [C]: using three independent methods on four West African populations, they inferred a ghost archaic lineage splitting 360,000 to 1.02 million years ago (mean 625 ka), admixing 0–124 thousand years ago (mean 43 ka), contributing 2–19 percent of ancestry (mean ~11 percent, confidence interval 4.5–19). Some 6.6–7 percent of Yoruba and Mende genomes was putatively archaic in their analysis, with loci such as NF1, MTFR2, HSD17B2, KCNIP4, and TRPS1 at striking frequencies. Two corrections belong in any honest account: the "19 percent" figure is the upper bound of a very wide confidence interval, not the estimate; and the lead author has since clarified publicly that the best-fitting version of the model has the signal shared by *all* populations — which is precisely what TRACE confirmed in 2026. When you see the "Africans are 19% archaic" claim online, you are watching a documented misinformation event in action, including the researcher's own attempts to correct it.

Finally, Roger and colleagues (2020) described the deepest layer of all: the Neanderthal–Denisovan ancestral population ("Neandersovan") met and absorbed a **super-archaic** population separated from it by at least 2 million years — and a second event around 350,000 years ago put erectus-like DNA into the Denisovan lineage, making the Denisovan and that super-archaic pair "the most distantly related interbreeders known" [S — *Science Advances* 2026 update confirms via TRACE].

The defensible summary: Homo sapiens carries at least four layers of admixture — recent structure (Part Two), Neanderthal and Denisovan pulses (Part Eight), and now a universal ~830,000-year-old ghost plus an erectus-grade layer in Oceania. Every named and unnamed human lineage of the ice age appears to have been somebody else's refugee.

## 2.4 Toba: the catastrophe that wasn't

Every textbook once told it: 74,000 years ago, the Toba super-eruption in Sumatra — a VEI-7 event, the largest explosive eruption of the Quaternary — blotted out the sun, triggered a volcanic winter, and nearly exterminated modern humans, leaving perhaps 1,000 to 15,000 survivors in tropical refugia. The story came from Stanley Ambrose's 1998 "Weak Garden of Eden" model [historically influential; now C], and it survived for two decades on the plausibility of catastrophe rather than the strength of evidence.

It is dead, and the way it died is a lesson in how science actually works:

1. **The ash traveled; the winter didn't.** Lake Malawi, 7,000 kilometers west of Toba, contains Toba's microscopic ash fingerprint — cryptotephra — in its sediments, and absolutely no accompanying sedimentological or temperature anomaly. The largest eruption of the age left no detectable climate trace in eastern Africa [S — Lane et al., *PNAS* 2013].
2. **The sulfur was overestimated.** The volcanic-winter models (Rampino & Self's lineage) injected one to two orders of magnitude too much sulfur dioxide; the corrected simulations cannot produce a global winter at all [S — Yost et al., *Journal of Human Evolution* 2018, "A falsified Toba catastrophe hypothesis"].
3. **The cooling started before the eruption.** Northern Hemisphere cold was already underway — the GS-20 stadial had begun — centuries before Toba erupted, so the post-eruption cold spike that anchored the catastrophe narrative was, in large part, a coincidence misread as a cause.
4. **A state-of-the-art climate model refused to cooperate.** Robock's 2009 simulation injected nine hundred times the Pinatubo forcing and still failed to initiate glaciation.
5. **The people didn't blink.** At Jwalapuram in southern India, occupation surfaces are sealed *below* the Toba ash (YTT) and used again above it; the 2025–26 re-analysis of the sequence resolved seven ash sub-units — one primary airfall and six rapidly remobilized by monsoonal action — and found **regional surface warming after the event** [S — Petraglia et al. 2007 *Science*; PMC12008719]. At Dhaba on the Middle Son River (Clarkson's team), stone tools continue through the ash without interruption. In Sumatra itself, human teeth from Lida Ajer date to 73–63 thousand years old — people living in closed rainforest in the eruption's aftermath. And at Pinnacle Point on the South African coast, the very populations the hypothesis said were nearly exterminated were thriving, harvesting shellfish and heating their stone.
6. **The ice cores agree.** The Greenland GISP2 record shows the sulfate spike as a six-year event; the layer-counting age (71.1±5 ka) and speleothem correlation (74.2±1.7 ka) bracket the eruption without drama.

The sociology deserves a line of its own: Michael Balter's 2010 *Science* essay "Of Two Minds About Toba's Impact" documented the two camps — the Ambrose school's catastrophe narrative against the Petraglia school's continuity evidence — and the genetic verdict has since landed on one side: **no bottleneck signal exists at 74 ka in any modern genomic analysis**. The correct statement is the opposite of the myth: *the largest eruption of the age failed to leave a demographic fingerprint anywhere we can measure.* Humanity's real bottleneck stories — the out-of-Africa founder event, the Beringian standstill, the LGM contractions — are documented in Parts Three through Eight. Toba was never one of them. Its afterlife in popular books is a standing lesson in how slowly the knowledge ecosystem corrects itself — and why this document tags claims instead of trusting reputations.

## 2.5 The doors of Arabia: exits before the exit

If modern humans evolved roughly 300,000 years ago in Africa, when did we first leave? The honest answer: repeatedly, and mostly unsuccessfully. Arabia is the key, because Arabia is not a desert that was always a desert. It is a **pump** — green, lake-dotted, and full of game during every warm orbital phase, then a sand sea in between — and its occupation ledger is now one of the best-kept records in Paleolithic science [S — the Palaeodeserts project, MPG/ Oxford; satellite surveys (Breeze's census) count roughly ten thousand ancient lake basins, 96 percent of the surveyed ones confirmed, with paleodrainage lines matching 86 percent of the modern network].

The ledger of confirmed or claimed presences, oldest first:

- **400,000 years ago**: Khall Amayshan 4 in the Nefud Desert preserves six stacked lake beds; five of them carry stone tools at 400, 300, 200, 100, and 55 thousand years ago — with elephants and hippos in attendance — and the site documents, in place, the transition from Acheulean handaxe technology to Middle Paleolithic flake industries: the first such sequence demonstrated in Arabia [S — Groucutt, White, Scerri et al., *Nature* 597:376, 2021]. Jubbah oasis, 150 kilometers east, adds 200 ka and 75 ka occupations.
- **~254,000 years ago**: a shell midden at Benzú, near Ceuta on the African shore of the Strait of Gibraltar, attests to systematic marine exploitation — the oldest on the African side [A].
- **177,000–194,000 years ago**: the **Misliya maxilla** — a partial upper jaw with a complete modern dental pattern (no shoveling, no Carabelli cusp; the Vienna team's microCT reconstruction under Gerhard Weber), directly dated by uranium-series, combined U-series/ESR on the teeth (Duval's low-destruction protocol), and thermoluminescence on thirty-two burnt flints — found with hearths (one large and repeatedly used), aurochs, Persian fallow deer, gazelle, marine shells, and charred grass tissues interpreted as bedding or matting: the benchmark early-exit fossil, and the earliest evidence of anything like a mattress [S — Hershkovitz et al., *Science* 359:456, 2018, cited 672 times; the jaw was found in 2002, announced in 2018, its layer sealed by a ceiling collapse ~160 ka]. Its excavators' corollary — that *Homo sapiens* itself must therefore be 300,000–500,000 years old — remains argued; the fossil itself is widely accepted.
- **~130,000–92,000 years ago**: sapiens at Skhul and Qafzeh in the Levant — and then **nothing** for over forty thousand years. The Levantine record itself records the failed wave.
- **125,000 years ago**: Jebel Faya in the UAE produced tools argued to mark a southern-route exit [C — Armitage et al., *Science* 331:453, 2011, single-grain OSL]. The case has since been revised downward: the 2023–2026 re-excavation revealed **six occupation phases running from 170,000 to 80,000 years ago**, with technological *continuity* across the sequence — the supposed 125 ka "African signature" weakened to species-neutral, in Bretzke and Herkert's metric words, "we do not see a significant change in the sense of an introduction of a developmentally unrelated technology." The site is real, deep, and human (likely); its early *species* is no longer established. UNESCO listed it as World Heritage in 2025 regardless — cultural politics, noted.
- **~120,000 years ago (MIS 5e)**: the Alathar lake in the Nefud preserved human footprints alongside elephant, horse, and camel — Arabia's last-interglacial refugium, documented on the ground [S — Stewart et al., *Science Advances* 2020].
- **85,000–95,000 years ago**: the **Al Wusta finger bone** — an intermediate phalanx, directly dated by uranium-series to at least 85 ka (Bayesian 95–86), found in a lake bed with primarily African fauna, a hippo tooth dated by ESR, and 380 stone tools: the oldest *directly dated* sapiens fossil outside Africa and the Levant [S — Groucutt et al., *Nature Ecology & Evolution* 2:800, 2018, cited 257 times; critics Weinstein-Evron and Hershkovitz note the bone's surface context, and Katerina Harvati urges caution on phalanx shape — kept in the file].

And then the pattern repeats: after each green phase, the doors closed. The wave ledger — 400, 300, 200, 130–120 (Alathar), 100, 85 (Al Wusta), 75 (Jubbah), 55 (Khall Amayshan's youngest lake) — ends with the 60–50 ka exodus that actually peopled the world. That final exit was not the first door Arabia opened. It was the last.

## 2.6 The failed-wave template

This is one of the organizing insights of modern paleogenomics, and it is worth stating as a law of the deep past: **early exits kept happening, and early exits kept failing.** The genetic record of the first modern humans in Eurasia reads like a cemetery of almost-rans:

- **Ust'-Ishim** (Siberia, 45,000 years old): a complete early genome, equidistant from all non-Africans, contributing detectably to no later population.
- **Oase** (Romania): early, admixed with Neanderthals within a few generations, a dead end.
- **Zlatý kůň** (Czechia): the longest Neanderthal segments then known, ~3.2 percent archaic ancestry, a lineage that left no descendants (its skull, incidentally, contaminated early DNA work with cattle-bone conservation glue — a museum cautionary tale, Part Eight).
- **Bacho Kiro** (Bulgaria) and the **Ranis** individuals (Germany): entire small populations that the 2025 genome paper from Ranis and Zlatý kůň closed the case on — the corollary stated bluntly: any modern human fossil found outside Africa older than 50,000 years "could not have been part of the common non-African population" [S — Sümer et al., *Nature*, doi 10.1038/s41586-024-08420-x]. That study also recovered the failed wave's human face: a mother and daughter buried together at Ranis, and fifth-to-sixth-degree relatives spanning Ranis and Zlatý kůň — one family scattered across 230 kilometers of a world about to disappear.

The template is older than the genomes. The Levant holds sapiens at Skhul and Qafzeh by 130–92 ka and then nothing for forty millennia. Greece's Apidima Cave yielded a ~210,000-year-old partial skull claimed as the oldest sapiens outside Africa [C — Harvati et al., *Nature* 571:500, 2019]: Apidima 1 is a posterior cranium only — a rounded occiput without the Neanderthal chignon, "a mixture of modern human and primitive features" — found in one breccia block with Apidima 2, a 170,000-year-old unmistakably Neanderthal face (66 fragments digitally reassembled), both dislodged from their original context with no archaeology attached. The critics are named and weighty: dating authority Warren Sharp notes the same sample returned ages from over 300 ka to under 40 ka — "not a well-behaved sample"; Susan Antón calls it plausible but no slam dunk, since the back of the skull does not predict the face (Jebel Irhoud, after all, pairs an archaic occiput with a modern face); Juan Luis Arsuaga "cannot see anything suggesting that it belongs to the sapiens lineage"; Henry de Lumley classes both skulls as an evolved "anteneandertal" grade (the Sima/Swanscombe/Biache class); even co-author Chris Stringer lists the missing pieces — no frontal bone, brow, face, teeth, or chin. Harvati's model, whatever the identification: a three-population relay at a single cave — sapiens at 210 ka, Neanderthals at 170 ka, sapiens again at 40 ka — with southeast Europe as the crossroads refuge where three continents' refugees met at the height of the ice ages.

So before we ever reach the last ice age, the deep past has taught its first lesson, which the rest of this document will keep proving: *humans have always been refugees; most refugee waves die; the survivors are the ones who found the refuges — and the ones whose genes, by luck of merger, made it into the braid.*

# PART THREE — THE PEOPLES BEFORE THE ICE (50,000–26,500 years ago)

---

## 3.1 The Initial Upper Palaeolithic arc: a cold-steppe civilization before the cold

By about 45,000 years ago, modern humans held an arc of settlements across the mid-latitudes of Eurasia, and the isotopic record shows they had already solved the cold. At **Ranis** in Germany — the type site of the Lincombian-Ranisian-Jerzmanowician (LRJ) blade industry — serial isotope analysis of sixteen horse teeth spanning 12,500 years shows people enduring temperatures **7 to 15°C below modern, with sub-freezing annual means**, on an open steppe of hypergrazers, in small mobile groups that apparently did not even hunker down in the coldest intervals [S — Pederzani et al., *Nature Ecology & Evolution* 8:578, 2024]. Cold resilience, on this evidence, came with the *first* dispersals into Europe, not later; the LRJ's leaf points, partially and fully bifacial, link Moravia to eastern Poland, the Rhineland — and, as we will see, Britain. Sediment DNA from Zavala cave adds an independent line.

The genomes of this world [S — Hajdinjak et al., *Nature* 592:253, 2021; Sikora et al. 2017; Prüfer et al. 2021; and related papers]:

- **Bacho Kiro Cave, Bulgaria (~45,000 years)**: the three main individuals carry 3–3.8 percent Neanderthal DNA, and their pedigree reconstructions place Neanderthal ancestors within roughly **six generations** — the closest actual meeting of the species ever sequenced. They belong to mitochondrial haplogroups M, N, R, and U8 and Y-haplogroup C1a2 — the latter rare today and *extinct*, though still present at Dolní Věstonice ten thousand years later. Bacho Kiro ancestry contributed substantially to later East Asians and Native Americans (the 40,000-year-old Tianyuan genome of China derives up to 39 percent from this lineage) and about 19 percent to the GoyetQ116-1 individual of Belgium — but only marginally to later Europeans. The cave also yielded pendants made from cave-bear teeth, a taste shared with the Neanderthals they were replacing.
- **Zlatý kůň, Czechia (~45,000 years)**: a woman with some of the longest Neanderthal segments ever measured, whose skull was once glued with cattle-bone adhesive that contaminated the first DNA attempts [S — Prüfer et al., *Nature Ecology & Evolution* 5:820, 2021]. Her mitochondrial lineage is basal N — closest to Bacho Kiro and to the Salkhit skull of Mongolia — a maternal signature that would span the entire IUP arc from Bulgaria to east Asia.
- **Kostenki 14, Russia (38,700–36,200 years)**: genetically close to Mal'ta, Sungir, and the later Mesolithic hunter-gatherers, carrying what looks like a basal component related to early European farmers — and the paper's anchor result: the last common genetic ancestor of western and eastern Eurasians lived *before* 36,200 years ago, with more Neanderthal ancestry sitting in longer tracts (an admixture ~54 thousand years back) [S — Seguin-Orlando et al., *Science* 346:1113, 2014].
- **Shiyu, northern China (45,800–43,200 years)**: a blade-and-Levallois assemblage with tanged points showing impact fractures (hafted and used), obsidian sourced from **800 to 1,000 kilometers away** (northeast China and the Russian Far East), a perforated graphite disk, and selective culling of adult horses: a full Upper Palaeolithic hunting system on the eve of the LGM [C — Yang et al., *Nature Ecology & Evolution* 8:293, 2024. The attribution is fought: John Shea of Stony Brook offers the alternative that Denisovans or Neanderthals adopted blade technology (and that a killed wanderer's bones could explain a lone human cranial fragment now lost from the 1963 collection); Bruno Teyssandier reads "most artifacts look just like Middle Paleolithic" and suspects layer mixing; the excavators answer with two concordant dating programs and rapid single-unit deposition, funded by NSFC, Norway's SapienCE centre, and an ERC grant — the money trail runs three continents].
- **Zhoukoudian Upper Cave, China (35,100–33,500 years, re-dated)**: three skulls — a "gracile" woman, an older man, a third with possible artificial head-shaping — buried with over 120 perforated canine teeth, hematite powder, and shell beads; and every original specimen **lost in 1941**, carted away with the Peking Man fossils in the chaos of the Japanese advance, so all study now happens on casts. Weidenreich's famous three-"race" reading of the crania was demolished by later geometric-morphometric work, which found the Upper Cave people unlike any recent East Asians; the classic East Asian cranial pattern is only securely present by the Neolithic, at Baoji (~7 ka) [S — Li et al. 2018; Harvati 2009].

The wider lesson: this was a *connected* world. The same maternal lineage spans Bulgaria to Mongolia. Obsidian moved a thousand kilometers. A hunting technology spanned from Thuringia to Shanxi. The ice age would soon test exactly this kind of connectivity — and where it survived, people survived. Where it failed, the cemeteries of Part Two's failed waves filled.

## 3.2 The Gravettian world: one culture, two peoples

From about 33,000 to 24,000 years ago, the **Gravettian** technocomplex stretched from Spain to Russia — similar backed points, similar hunting economies, similar art and burials across an entire continent. Ancient DNA has now split that unity in two. Posth and colleagues (*Nature*, 2023, cited 400+ times) showed the Gravettian was made by two genetically distinct populations that had apparently adopted one culture without merging: a **western cluster** ("Fournol," from Spain–France, ~32–21 ka, genetically continuous with the 35,000-year-old GoyetQ116-1 individual) and an **eastern cluster** ("Věstonice," Italy–Czechia–Russia, ~31–29 ka, related to Kostenki and Sungir), with the Crimean site of Buran-Kaya III (~36–37 ka) sitting with the western cluster — an eastern outpost of the Fournol world [S].

Around 37,000 years ago the climate tipped into a long deterioration after the last warm event, the steppe shifted toward tundra, and the Gravettian network contracted and fragmented — the first demonstration, on a continental scale, of what the D-O rhythm would keep doing to human connections (Part Four). A dental-phenomic study of 450 Upper Palaeolithic dentitions adds a provocative twist [S — Rathmann et al., *Science Advances* 2024]: the best-fitting model has the *west* replaced from the *east* around 28,000 years ago, with eastern and western populations then isolated through the late glacial and exchanging genes again only after 14,700 years ago — partly contradicting the genomic picture of southwestern continuity (the Solutrean–Malalmuerzo line, Part Five). Both may be partly right: genes and teeth measure different histories, and southwestern Iberia may have kept its people while losing its neighbors' connections. The Fournol cluster's fate would be decided in exactly that corner of Europe.

## 3.3 Sungir and Mal'ta: the two poles of the pre-LGM north

Two burial complexes define the spiritual and genetic poles of the world before the ice.

**Sungir**, 200 kilometers east of Moscow, ~34,000 years old [S — Trinkaus et al., *Antiquity* 2018, cited 106; Sikora et al., *Science* 358:659, 2017, cited 446; the site, found by quarrymen in 1955 and excavated across 1957–77 by Otto Bader, sits on a ridge above the Klyazma]. A 35-to-45-year-old man died suddenly — a blade incision into his first thoracic vertebra that severed the spinal cord — and was buried with about 3,000 ivory beads sewn to his clothing, twelve pierced fox canines across his forehead, twenty-five ivory armbands, and a schist pendant. Nearby, two children — ten and twelve years old, not siblings despite sharing mitochondrial DNA — were laid head-to-head with roughly 5,000 and 5,400 beads each (two-thirds adult size), forty-odd fox canines, ivory fibulae across their chests like cape fasteners, and **sixteen mammoth-ivory spears up to 2.47 meters long**, five double-pointed, arranged so the longest stretched from one child's feet to the other's pelvis. A quadruped pendant and a large mammoth carving completed the grave goods, and a separate deposit — Grave 2bis — held worked tusks, fossil mollusc shells, and reindeer-antler clubs unique to the site. A fourth element is the strangest: a femur shaft whose epiphyses were removed and medullary cavity packed with ochre — and whose elemental profile differs from the other three, meaning it belonged to a person of a *different region*, carried or curated from afar. In total: some 13,400 beads, an estimated **ten thousand person-hours of production**, for children one of whom was bedridden with congenital illness, dental malformation, and healed nutritional stress — fed soft food for years. Deep social care, on this evidence, predates the ice age's worst by ten millennia. In richness, only Brno 2 and Ostuni 1 (600–700 beads each) even approach Sungir; the rest of the Upper Palaeolithic record is poorer by an order of magnitude.

Genetically, the five Sungir genomes (coverage 1.11–10.75x; all male — one individual long sexed as female from morphology; none closely related beyond third degree, despite the shared graves; effective population ~200–500, right in the range of modern isolated foragers) tell a population story: Neanderthal admixture dated to 53.6–58.1 thousand years ago, a Y-chromosome lineage C1a2 with no modern descendants, and a population *descended from people related to Kostenki 14 who had partially replaced the earlier Kostenki population* — with about 2 percent Tianyuan-related ancestry folded in, the earliest measurable west-east gene flow in the west. The exogamy implied by the unrelated co-buried — mates sought outside the band, across a multilevel mating network — is the social structure that large-spectrum networks like Gravettian exchange would rest on.

**Mal'ta–Buret'**, on the Angara River in Siberia, ~24,000 years old [S — Raghavan et al., *Nature* 505:87, 2014, PMC4105016; excavated by Mikhail Gerasimov in 1928]: a three-to-four-year-old boy buried under a stone slab with a one-to-two-year-old companion, ochre, and grave goods that read like a treasury: a mammoth-ivory diadem or tiara, a necklace of one central, six secondary, and 120 flat beads, an ivory bracelet, a bird-shaped pendant, a zigzag-decorated plaque, an ivory rod, a flint knife — and a crown? The boy's head carried an elaborate ivory headdress. The site itself is a residential base: circular stone-lined dwellings with central hearths and storage pits, tens of thousands of artifacts, over 500 worked bone items, some thirty ivory female figurines, and a faunal list that is the mammoth steppe in miniature (reindeer, arctic fox, woolly rhino, mammoth, bison, wolverine, red deer, cave lion, brown bear, wolf, large fish). The boy is the founding genome of the **Ancient North Eurasian (ANE)** population — a lineage of enormous later consequence (Part Eight): draft genome at 1x (the oldest modern-human genome at the time), mitochondrial U at 76-fold coverage fitting no known subclade, Y chromosome basal R, and the estimation that would echo for a decade: 14–38 percent of Native American ancestry derives from a Mal'ta-related source. The nearby Afontova Gora-2 individual (~17 ka) would later prove the region was continuously occupied through the LGM.

Between Sungir and Mal'ta lies roughly 5,000 kilometers, and they are genetically related. The same ivory-bead funerary grammar, the same colossal investment in children's graves, spans that distance. Whatever the ice age was about to do, it was doing it to people who already treated each other's children as worth ten thousand hours.

## 3.4 The Venus barometer

More than two hundred **Venus figurines** — portable statuettes of women, carved in mammoth ivory, limestone, serpentine, and fired clay — are known from the European Upper Palaeolithic, and their geography is a map of glacial stress. Richard Johnson and colleagues proposed in 2020 that they are a climate instrument [S — *Obesity* 28:943, PMC7902358, cited 37]: obesity indices (waist-to-shoulder and waist-to-hip ratios) are highest in figurines from near the glacial front, during glacial advance between 38,000 and 22,000 years ago, and turn leaner after the LGM ends — a cultural ideal tracking nutritional stress, in which stored fat signaled survival and fertility assurance in famine-prone times, as temperatures fell 4–8°C from ~28 ka and coldest months hit −10 to −15°C, and as big game was replaced by small (hare, rabbit, marmot, birds). The study's roster marches with the ice: Hohle Fels 38 ka (the oldest), Willendorf 28 ka, Dolní Věstonice 26 ka (fired clay — among the oldest ceramics on Earth, Part Nine), Kostenki 25 ka, Mal'ta 23 ka, Gagarino 22 ka, Avdeevo 20 ka, Zaraysk 19 ka, Eliseevichi 15 ka, Gönnersdorf and Petersfels 13–11 ka; and the two figurines the team verified from private collections (a black basalt Willendorf-type piece >30 ka; a Lespuge-type piece ~25 ka from the Pyrenean glacial margin) show the tradition ran right along the ice edge.

The eastern province is the equal of the western: the **Kostenki–Avdeevo cultural sphere** of the Russian plain produced the Zaraysk Venuses — two ivory figures, one 16.6 cm and possibly pregnant, found in 2005 under a mammoth scapula on an ochre lens 11 by 4 centimeters, in a pit a third full when deposited: deliberate ritual placement (Amirkhanov & Lev, *Antiquity* 2008) — plus the Avdeevo double-Venus buried back-to-back with a full toolkit (Gvozdover's "ritual-magical operations"), the Khotylevo chalk double-figure over its ochre horizon (Gavrilov's excavations), and the Ukrainian mammoth-bone art of Mezin and Mezhirich (Part Five). A parallel tradition flourished at the other end of the world: Sulawesi's Leang Bulu Bettue yielded two engraved plaquettes (26–14 ka) — a sun-and-eye motif and an anoa buffalo — the first Pleistocene portable art of Southeast Asia, interpreted as "social glue" for groups under harsher conditions [S — Brumm et al., *Nature Human Behaviour* 2020; ARC Future Fellowship-funded].

The synthesis writes itself: where the ice stood closest, people made the densest corpus of survival symbolism yet found — at both ends of the inhabited world, independently.

## 3.5 The other humans' last world

While sapiens organized this world, the Neanderthals were finishing theirs. They were the ice age's senior citizens — a lineage that emerged in Europe's Middle Pleistocene cold and endured half a dozen glacial cycles before we arrived — and their last ten millennia are a map of contraction. The ROAD and ROACEEH databases track their sites spreading from Iberia to the Black Sea between 130,000 and 80,000 years ago, then contracting to a possible southwestern refugium by 70–60,000 years ago, in the cold of MIS 4; 2026 reporting frames the endgame as "the last Neanderthals descended from a single refugium population," with continuous western-Mediterranean occupation through MIS 6 and contraction at the HS11 cold snap (~133–129 ka) already visible [S-adjacent — database syntheses; the single-refugium claim is fresh and C-graded].

At **Gibraltar**, they held a unique promontory: the coastal plain extended five kilometers east of the Rock (now drowned, identified by marine survey), rainfall was higher and winters milder than the glaciated interior — the herpetofaunal climate reconstruction puts the latest Pleistocene only 1.6–1.8°C below modern, with four dry months instead of five — and the caves preserve a hundred-millennia record: rock engravings older than 39,000 years (the cross-hatched Gorham's floor, analyzed by Finlayson's team), cut-marked bird wing bones (feather use), and marine harvesting of dolphins, seals, and limpets on a scale unmatched elsewhere in their world. Claims that Gibraltar hosted the *last* Neanderthals, surviving to 28,000 or even 24,000 years ago, remain contested [C]; the more secure late refuges include the Zagros (Shanidar, to ~45 ka), the northwest Caucasus (Mezmaiskaya, with its two culturally distinct populations — Part Eleven), and the Altai (Chagyrskaya: an isolated community of fewer than sixty individuals hunting bison by seasonal migration).

At Shanidar, the record already holds both poles of the encounter to come: a one-armed, partially blind, deaf man with eleven documented pathologies surviving at least a decade on provisioning — the strongest single case for Neanderthal care (Part Nine details the surgery hypothesis) — and one of his neighbors, Shanidar 3, dying of a low-energy projectile wound to the ribs, a weapon type associated with modern humans: the strongest candidate for lethal interspecies violence [S — Churchill et al. 2009].

And in the high valleys of Georgia, a new population was already in place: the **Dzudzuana** shelter, occupied by modern humans from about 34,500 years ago — the people whose genomes may anchor the entire post-ice-age Near East (Part Five) — while Neanderthals held parts of the northern Caucasus until roughly 45–40,000 years ago. The contact-zone mosaic was already forming: two species of human sharing a mountain range, one retreating upslope in time as well as space, one arriving. The Caucasus, one of the great refugium systems of the ice age, would be contested ground for another twenty millennia.

# PART FOUR — THE LAST GLACIAL MAXIMUM (26,500–19,000 years ago)

---

## 4.1 The frozen planet

The numbers first, because they are hard to feel [S — EPILOG synthesis, Clark et al., *Quaternary Science Reviews* 2002; Lambeck et al., *PNAS* 2014; USGS compilations]:

- **Sea level**: 120–135 meters below today. The EPILOG consortium bracketed the eustatic lowstand at 118 to 130–135 m; ice-sheet models cluster at 112–124 m plus about 6 m from small peripheral caps; coral reefs at Barbados record at least −120 m at 19 ka. Lambeck's best solution puts the lowest stand, **−134 meters, at about 21,000 years ago**, with roughly 52 million cubic kilometers of extra grounded ice on the planet.
- **Ice**: about 8% of Earth's surface, 25% of its land, and 33% of Alaska under ice (today: 3%, 11%). The Laurentide ice sheet over Canada reached 3.2–3.5 kilometers over Keewatin; its four domes (Keewatin, Foxe, Hudson, Québec) merged by 60 ka and pushed south to the Missouri and Ohio rivers — and over Manhattan. The Cordilleran and Innuitian sheets, Greenland, and the coalescent Kara Sea margin (smaller than the old CLIMAP maximum, a detail that mattered for Beringia's width) completed the northern armor.
- **Air**: glacial atmospheric CO₂ ran 180–200 parts per million (against ~280 in interglacials; the Vostok ice cores hold the curve); windblown dust was twenty to twenty-five times modern levels, the hydrological cycle ran weak, and the glacial atmosphere was, by ice-core count, the dustiest our species ever breathed.
- **Deglaciation's shape**: the main melt ran 16,500–7,000 years ago at average rates near 12 meters of sea-level rise per millennium — with the catastrophic pulses of Part Six embedded in it.
- **Region by region**: eastern Beringia was cold and dry (July temperatures 2–3°C below today) but ice-free — a grass-and-sagebrush steppe with firewood; the American Southwest was paradoxically *wetter* and forested, its lakes full; southern Africa cooled ~6°C; Australia cooled ~10°C with rainfall down ~60% and its southwestern forests gone; the Korean peninsula was humid-cold (−5.85°C but only −14% precipitation — a crucial difference from the dry interiors, and the root of its refuge, Part Five); and on the Tibetan Plateau, the archaeological record shows a genuine gap — not a single firmly dated LGM site — between the late-MIS-3 blade camps and the deglacial microblade era: the highest refuge stood empty at the freeze's deepest point [S — Zhang et al., *Trends in Ecology & Evolution* 2021].

The harshest fact is a geometric one. Miikka Tallavaara and colleagues calculated that even at the coldest point, *36% of Europe remained climatically suitable for foragers* — unfragmented, habitable land, concentrated in Iberia and the Mediterranean [S — *PNAS* 2015, PMC4500234]. The LGM did not make Europe empty; it made it *narrow*. Survival became a problem of geography — and geography is what the refugia map (Part Five) records, refuge by refuge, with its verdicts.

## 4.2 The climate engine: Dansgaard–Oeschger and Heinrich

Greenland ice cores preserve the ice age's heartbeat in isotopic stereo: **Dansgaard–Oeschger (D-O) events** — twenty-five abrupt warmings during the last glacial, each 5–15°C over Greenland within years to decades, paced suspiciously close to a 1,470-year cycle, followed by slow centuries of cooling back into the cold [S — Rahmstorf, *Geophysical Research Letters* 30:1510, 2003, cited 328; the canonical age table, GISP2, years before present]:

| Event | Age (yr BP) | | Event | Age (yr BP) |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| DO-0 | 11,605 | | DO-6 | 33,581 |
| Allerød | 13,073 | | DO-7 | 35,270 |
| DO-1 | 14,630 | | DO-8 | 38,387 |
| DO-2 | 23,398 | | DO-10 | 41,143 |
| DO-3 | 27,821 | | DO-11 | 42,537 |
| DO-4 | 29,021 | | DO-12 | 45,362 |
| DO-5 | 32,293 | | | |

Eleven of the thirteen intervals fall within ten percent of multiples of the 1,470-year period — a pacemaker whose origin (solar, per Braun's model; ocean-internal, per the modelers) remains unresolved, and Ganopolski and Rahmstorf's stochastic-resonance models reproduce the rhythm with a noise-driven Atlantic circulation. The abruptness is the point: the Allerød began 2.74‰ of warming in 172 years; terminations ran 5–7°C in thirty to forty years; some steps show 8°C in forty years in three five-year jumps.

Superimposed on these warmings are **Heinrich events** — armadas of icebergs from the collapsing Laurentide, recorded as debris layers in Atlantic sediments, always arriving in the cold spell just before a D-O warming [S]. Heinrich 2 falls around 24,000–23,400 years ago; Heinrich 1 around 16,800. A 2024 modeling study (Max Planck Institute) resolved a long mystery — why the pan-ice-sheet collapses were synchronous across half a planet — by showing the ice sheets' own internal instability triggers them (the "binge-purge" cycle), with the timing *locked* to the D-O cooling phase through atmospheric perturbation: snowfall and surface temperature changes nudge all the sheets past their thresholds together [S — Schannwell, Mikolajewicz, Kapsch & Ziemen 2024]. During the coldest stadials, winter sea ice reached 40°N — the latitude of France and New York City — and the Nordic Seas alone, Buizert's 2024 spatial fingerprint showed, were too small to drive an event; the sea-ice displacement south of Iceland is the amplifier [S — *PNAS* 2024]. Heinrich events slow the Atlantic's overturning circulation almost to a stop — Greenland freezes while Antarctica warms, the "bipolar seesaw" — and the ice-rafted debris of successive events marches down the 1,470-year beat as Bond cycles. Global fingerprints: speleothems reorganize storm tracks (Fohlmeister 2023), the Black Sea's Sofular record shows sea-surface plunges at H5–H0 (Held 2024), and the Sahara pumps green and brown with the same clock (Part Eight's Green Sahara).

Here is why this machinery matters to human beings rather than only to oceanographers: **culture breathes with these pulses**. The Gravettian network broke down after the warmth of D-O 8 (38,387 years ago) gave way to cold. The Peña Capón exchange network of Solutrean Iberia (below) spans the cold of D-O 2 (23,398 years ago) — networks held through that stadial. The LGM itself sits between Heinrich 2 and the Bølling warming, at the very bottom of the curve. African refugia pulsed with the same clock: the "Green Sahara" switched on and off with precession on a 21,000-year beat, and meltwater physics (Part Six) turned the same switches. When you read the archaeology of the next three parts, keep the table from Section 1.2 beside you: nearly every expansion and collapse in human prehistory has a number in it.

## 4.3 The mammoth steppe: the engine room — and its murder mystery

The ice-age cold created the largest grassland biome of the Quaternary: the **mammoth steppe**, some 70 million square kilometers of grass-herb-worm tundra-steppe ringing the ice sheets from Spain to Canada [S — Zimov et al., *Quaternary Science Reviews* 2012, cited 400+]. Its herbivore biomass equaled an African savanna — Sergey Zimov estimates around ten tonnes per square kilometer in the Siberian yedoma regions, with no basal peat dates during the LGM (wetlands were rare) and atmospheric methane at roughly half-Holocene values, the herbivores themselves rivaling what all modern humans and their livestock produce today. The formation physics (Guthrie's synthesis): a moisture-blocking triad (winter-frozen North Atlantic, exposed shelves, ice-sheet barriers) starved the interior of snow and rain; low CO₂ favored grasses over shrubs; thin snow disadvantaged shrubs (herbs hold their biomass underground); and loess dust fertilized the whole system. This was the larder on which the Yana hunters, the Mal'ta people, the Mezhirich mammoth-house builders, and the first Americans all fed — and its collapse is the last ice age's great ecological whodunit:

- **Climate suspect**: Guthrie's model — post-glacial warming and wetting flipped grassland to shrub and bog; the food base vanished with or without hunters. Consistent with the peat and methane records.
- **Human suspect**: Zimov's overkill case — horse and bison date-densities vanish within about 300 years of 12,400 radiocarbon years ago, exactly when humans appear in the same record (12,370 14C BP); south of 73°N, mammoths cluster at archaeological sites (human-killed), north of them at natural death sites (climate-killed, surviving into the post-glacial Arctic).
- **The 2026 synthesis**: mammoths still lived at Volchia Griva on the West Siberian plain at 12,500 years ago, *during the Younger Dryas*, while pollen shows the habitat was still 40–60% open — too open for a pure climate kill, and too fast a decline for climate alone; the honest verdict is **synergy**: climate stress plus a new predator that did not need the habitat to degrade first [S — Kuzmin, Dobretsov & Lyamina, *Radiocarbon* 2026; earlier interglacials may have spared the megafauna precisely because the exposed shelves kept the interior dry — the Eemian Batagay section shows a more open Arctic than the Holocene].

The steppe's last pockets deserve their own paragraphs, because each is the LGM in miniature. At **Volchia Griva**, a mineral-lick oasis (a "beast solonetz") in the Baraba forest-steppe — Asia's largest *in situ* mammoth locality, over 9,000 bones across several hectares — the youngest southern mammoths died between 10,620 and 11,815 years ago, and people were there in the late LGM, 18,230–19,790 years ago, carrying finished blades from 170 to over 1,000 kilometers away because the Baraba plain has no stone at all; their industry includes backed micro-points and rock crystal geochemically excluded from all local sources (Kazakhstan or the southern Urals). In 2020, excavators found a hollowed mammoth femur — previously used as a flint retoucher — containing a fox cranium with skinning cut-marks, a fox jaw, a tooth, and a large-mammal rib: a ritual cache about 19,300 years old "with no known analogues." The Soviet-era museum pavilion was later dismantled and its exhibits lost. Farther north, **Berelekh** on the Yana-Indigirka lowland — over 9,000 mammoth bones, minimum 156–200 individuals, discovered in 1947 — was long read as a natural accumulation (Vereschagin's verdict). A 2024 re-analysis reversed it: the bone bed is human-made, accumulated over 800 years (13,700–11,800 radiocarbon BP), with blowfly pupae in skulls showing the bones arrived defleshed, valuable parts sorted and transported from kill sites, three accumulation peaks (12.4/12.2/11.8 ka), hare and fox fur for clothing, and an ivory-working industry — one to two mammoths killed, butchered, and macerated annually for their tusks, the steppe's post-glacial ivory economy [S — Pitulko et al., *Quaternary Science Research* 2024]. Its epitaph, from its excavator: the bone bed "no longer exists due to looting by modern-day mammoth ivory hunters... this is the end of the story."

And the final refuge: **Wrangel Island**, where mammoths persisted until about 3,700 years ago — isolated when the sea rose ~10,000 years ago, founded by perhaps eight individuals, recovering to a few hundred within twenty generations, accumulating mildly deleterious mutations while actively purging severe ones — and, per a 2024 genomic study of twenty-one individuals (fourteen Wrangel, seven mainland), **not genetically doomed at all**: a demographically stable population that a random event — a storm, a tundra fire, a pathogen, the poor DNA preservation of the last three centuries leaves the scene unwitnessed — finally killed [S — Dehasque et al., *Cell* 2024]. The conservation benchmarks of 500 individuals to retain diversity and 1,000 to prevent mutation accumulation would have called Wrangel unviable; it persisted for six thousand years anyway. The lesson cuts both ways for our own story: small refugee populations can persist for millennia — and "bottleneck" is a description, not a death sentence. (Also on the horse family tree: the Americas' stilt-legged horses were a separate genus, **Haringtonhippus** — born in America, convergently ass-like, surviving *through* the LGM in eastern Beringia to ~14.4 ka — extinguished within a millennium of first contact; Part Eight.)

## 4.4 How many people?

Estimating ice-age populations is a fight between methods, so carry all the numbers:

- **Climate-envelope modeling** (Tallavaara, Pesonen et al., *PNAS* 2015): calibrate modern hunter-gatherer densities against climate across 127 ethnographic groups, project backward. Result: Europe held roughly **330,000 people at 30,000 years ago**, fell to about **130,000 at the LGM minimum (23 ka)**, and rebounded to ~410,000 by 13,000 years ago — densities of 2.8–5.1 people per 100 km², fastest growth in the GI-1 warm spell. Even at the coldest, 36% of Europe stayed suitable and — the crucial finding — *unfragmented*. The model's validation is persuasive: it retrodicts pre-contact Australia's 780,000–800,000 people against estimates of 750,000–1.1 million.
- **Site-count methods** (Bocquet-Appel 2005): fewer than 6,000 in LGM Europe — an estimate Tallavaara's team dismantled on its site-visibility and single-density assumptions; a later "optimal isoline" variant yields 1,400–6,300 for 25–20 ka [C between schools — and a warning about how much demography rests on what we can see].
- **Dental phenomics** (Rathmann et al., *Science Advances* 2024; 450 dentitions, Pheno-ABC modeling): significant decline plus network breakdown in the late Gravettian, then bottlenecks on *both* sides of Europe during the LGM, exchange resuming only after 14.7 ka.
- **Global**: a machine-learning reconstruction of the pre-agricultural "hunter-gatherer planet" lands near **6.1 million ± 2 million** people worldwide [P — bioRxiv 2021, validated against contact-era Australia].

For scale: the entire human species at the LGM would fit inside a modern mid-sized city, scattered across all the habitable land of three continents — with the densest concentrations in the refuges of Part Five, and the best-connected ones (Part Four's closing section) the likeliest to survive.

## 4.5 The seven-hundred-kilometer handshake: Peña Capón

The most astonishing LGM discovery of the decade is a pile of stones in Guadalajara province, Spain. The Solutrean site of Peña Capón lies 600–700 kilometers (620–672 km by the geochemical matches) from the jasper sources of the Dordogne and Vienne — and laser-ablation ICP-MS geochemistry proved that its people carried tools from France across that distance, maintained the connection for **1,400 years spanning the cold of Heinrich Stadial 2 (~25,300–23,900 years ago)**, and organized the flow through a network covering up to 88,995 square kilometers at its second-order level — larger than any ethnographically recorded forager annual range (the Baffinland Inuit manage about 25,000 km²) [S — de la Torre, Sánchez-Romero et al., *Science Advances* 2026, PMC12822654; ERC MULTIPALEOIBERIA 805478, Alcaraz-Castaño].

The logistics are recoverable with fair precision: a 35.6-day round trip for the longest hauls; tools moved "down the line" through at least six adjacent maximal bands, most pieces carried unused — gifts, not provisions; and the math of gift obligation implies a safety net of allies stretching from the Cantabrian refuge to the Tagus basin. The network moved ideas too: El Reno cave, nine kilometers from Peña Capón, carries art motifs in the Dodeogne-Charente style. Central Iberia, on this evidence, was not a marginal crossing zone but a node.

This is the LGM as its inhabitants experienced it: not a wasteland, but a **community of communities holding hands across seven hundred kilometers of ice-age Europe** precisely when the climate was at its worst. The refugia of Part Five were never islands. That, more than any single site, is why some of them worked — and the thread runs unbroken from here to the Magdalenian re-expansion, the WHG expansion, and the modern distribution of Europe's maternal lineages.

# PART FIVE — THE REFUGIA MAP

## 5.1 Europe

**Refugium** (plural *refugia*): the place a population survives the worst part of a glacial cycle, from which it re-expands when conditions improve (formal definition adapted from Stewart et al. 2010). The map below is the heart of this document. For each region: what the refuge was, how we know, and what became of its people. Verdicts are graded: **CONFIRMED** (ancient DNA or equivalent direct evidence), **PROBABLE** (strong archaeology), **CONTESTED** (genuine dispute), and **UNTESTED** (hypothesis awaiting a test). Throughout, remember the rule established in Part One: humans show a weaker refugial signature than any other mammal — our refugia were connected or they were nothing.

### 5.1.1 The Franco-Cantabrian refuge — CONFIRMED

The Atlantic-facing arc of northern Spain and southwest France was the ice age's best-attested human refuge, and the case for it is a chain of direct evidence no other refuge can match. Genetically, the continuity runs unbroken across the freeze: the pre-LGM **Fournol** cluster → the Solutrean individual of Malalmuerzo Cave (~23 ka, who "links the groups before and after the LGM," in Vanessa Villalba-Mouco's phrase — *Nature Ecology & Evolution* 2023) → the Magdalenian expansion → the post-glacial western hunter-gatherers, with the El Mirón individual (~19 ka, already 43% Villabruna) as the bridge pulse [S — Posth et al., *Nature* 2023; the Oberkassel cluster models as ~75% Arene Candide + 25% GoyetQ2, or 90% Arene Candide + 10% Fournol, depending on model]. Sedimentary ancient DNA from Ebro Basin caves independently confirms the same thing from the dirt itself: part of the gene pool sat out the LGM in place, with continuity through the Solutrean 24,500–21,000 years ago [S — Gelabert et al., *Nature Communications* 2025; the same technique that later read Doggerland's rivers and Denisova's layers].

The maternal DNA still remembers the shape of the refuge: haplogroups H1 and H3 reach their world diversity peaks in Iberia [S — Achilli et al., *American Journal of Human Genetics* 2004]; HV4's root lies in eastern Europe ~14.2 ka, but **HV4a1a arose in the Franco-Cantabrian zone around 5,400 years ago, and 46% of everyone on Earth carrying it today descends from the Cantabrian cornice** — with Acadian and French-Canadian lineages tracing back to Bay of Biscay fishing ports [S — Gómez-Carballa et al., *PLoS ONE* 7:e32851, 2012]. The Basque country preserves six autochthonous maternal lineages (H1j1, H1t1, H2a5a1, H1av1, H3c2a, H1e1a1) read as continuity with pre-Neolithic settlers by their investigators [S — Pereira et al. 2012, "the Basque paradigm"], the same study dating their expansion to ~4,000 and their separation from the pan-European pool to ~8,000 years ago — a *small-survival* case, the remnant of a refuge, not the key to a continent (the over-extended version of that claim is dissected with its critics in Part Ten).

And the cost of the freeze is written in what did *not* survive: pre-LGM Europeans of Belgium and France carried mitochondrial haplogroup **M** lineages — today essentially absent in Europeans — lost in the contraction [S — Posth et al. 2016]. The refuge kept a fraction of the old world, and lost the rest; the Gravettian world of Part Three survives in Iberia's genes and in Belgium's archaeology, but nowhere whole.

### 5.1.2 Italy — CONFIRMED as refuge, DEAD END as lineage

Italy sheltered people through the LGM — the Epi-Gravettian stretches from the Adriatic to Sicily, and the peninsula's glacial refugium status was never in doubt — but ancient DNA delivered the decade's rudest surprise: the post-LGM Italians are **not** descended from the pre-LGM Gravettian inhabitants. The earlier population was replaced; the new one arrived from the Balkans. The Tagliente mandible (northeast Italy, 16,980–16,510 cal BP) catches the migration in the act: a Villabruna-linked, Balkan-refugium component present 3,000 years before the conventional Bølling warm-up, moving *during* the cold, accompanied by a genetic turnover at the Early-to-Late Epigravettian transition — demic diffusion, not just diffusion of ideas [S; the mandible also preserves a rare focal osseous dysplasia, a window on Upper Palaeolithic lives]. The 33,000-year-old Paglicci genome carries a faint signal of gene flow into southern Italy from earlier times — a whisper of the people being replaced. The Iconic **Villabruna** individual himself (14 ka, Y-haplogroup R1b1, mitochondrial U5b) would father the entire post-glacial west.

The islands fill out the file: the San Teodoro burials in Sicily (15.3–14.4 ka) — a woman whose protein-residue diet included common carp and small shark, a man among ibex, both from the post-LGM Italian gene pool, the woman carrying mitochondrial U5b2b, a lineage whose divergence (~23 ka) sits exactly in the LGM window [S — PMC9674856]. Italy's role in the story is thus the reverse of the classic peninsular-refuge script: not the source of the recolonizers, but the *meeting ground* where the Balkan stream met whatever the ice had left behind.

### 5.1.3 The Balkans — CONFIRMED as the engine

The 2026 preprint that finally sequenced the Balkan refuge rewrote the map [P, influential: "The Pan-European Impact of the Balkan Hunter-Gatherers," bioRxiv 10.64898/2026.08.05.743150, posted August 2026; nine new genomes — three Epipaleolithic Romanians, one Bosnian, three Mesolithic Greeks, one Epipaleolithic southern Italian, one Mesolithic Sardinian — from a consortium spanning the Fred Hutchinson Cancer Center, Imperial College, MIT, Stanford, and Edinburgh, with European partners]. For thirty years the Balkans were the missing third leg of the European refuge system: archaeology said Epi-Gravettian, but no genomes existed between 30 and 12 ka. Now:

- A distinct **Epi-Balkan** population emerges, genetically close to but distinct from the Epi-Italian (the renamed western hunter-gatherer) lineage.
- **Italy's post-LGM people are ~75% Balkan + ~20% pre-LGM Italian + ~5% pre-LGM Spanish-related.** The WHG expansion was substantially a Balkan export, with Italy as the admixture zone — matching, at last, the archaeology of Epi-Gravettian replacement.
- The expansion ran in **four vectors**: west into Italy (above); farther west, with Iberian hunter-gatherers gaining Balkan ancestry over time; **northeast, bypassing Italy entirely** into the Scandinavian and eastern hunter-gatherers; and **southeast into the Aegean**, seeding Anatolian-European mixtures *before* farming arrived.
- The long reach: the eastern hunter-gatherers (part-ANE, Part Eight) carry the Balkan component, and **Yamnaya is ~16% Epi-Balkan** — the refuge that seeded the steppe got the last word when the steppe's descendants rode back across Europe and into Central and South Asia in the Bronze Age.
- Y-chromosome snapshot of the refuge's sons: R1b-L754 (two Romanians), H-P96, I-M436 (Greece), I-S2555 (Sardinia) — the paternal diversity of half the post-glacial west, already present in a handful of men.

Parallel to it, the **Sidelkino** lineage of the Russian plain (~13–6 ka) held the eastern hunter-gatherer pole — dark eyes, light skin, from a different part of the post-glacial mosaic — isolated from Oberkassel's west for six millennia until the two began mixing around 8 ka [S — Posth 2023]. The refuge everyone dismissed as an archaeological backwater was, genetically, the *source population of half the modern world*. This is the single largest revision in European ice-age studies since the first ancient genomes — and, like Dzudzuana below, it arrived by preprint, which is why this document tags it [P].

### 5.1.4 Fennoscandia — the last place ice-free Europe was claimed

Scandinavia was deglaciated so late that its pioneers arrived while the ice still stood in the mountains, on a coast rising from the sea faster than any memory could stabilize. Genomics caught two migrations [S — Günther et al., *PLoS Biology* 2018, PMC5760011; seven genomes, 9.5–6 ka, up to 57x coverage]: western hunter-gatherers arriving from the south (blue eyes, dark skin, direct-percussion blade technology, ties to the Ahrensburgian of Denmark and Germany) and eastern hunter-gatherers moving up the ice-free Norwegian Atlantic coast (paler skin, varied eye color, pressure-flaked microblade technology), mixing into Scandinavian hunter-gatherers with an east-west gradient *opposite* to the rest of Europe. They carried elevated light-pigmentation alleles beyond what either source population had — selection working within centuries — and a cold-adapted variant near TMEM131 that remains among the strongest continuity signals in northern Europeans today. At least 17% of the SNPs common in these hunter-gatherers have since vanished from Europeans entirely: the north was later swamped harder than anywhere. Isotope ratios show the western and northern foragers lived off an extreme maritime diet, Ertebølle-style, while eastern and central groups mixed terrestrial and aquatic foods; one woman's face has been reconstructed in 3D from her genome.

Archaeology now says even the two-migration model is too simple [S — *Antiquity* 2025, "First encounters in the north"]: at least **six immigration events** before 7,500 BC, the first before 9,000 BC, mapped with 572 new radiocarbon dates and 205 shoreline dates. Parducci's spruce-genetics work suggests the trees, at least, survived the LGM in ice-free coastal pockets of western Norway — haplotypes found nowhere else, chloroplast DNA in lake sediments dated to 17.7 ka — and if conifers held those pockets, the question of human cryptic refugia north of the ice stays formally open, though no human evidence exists and the mammal record argues against [S — Parducci et al., *Science* 335:1083, 2012, with its same-issue critique].

### 5.1.5 The Ukrainian bone-house province

North of the Black Sea, the Epigravettian peoples of the Dnieper valley built the ice age's most spectacular architecture: houses framed from the bones of dozens of mammoths. **Mezhirich** (~15 ka; found in 1965 by a farmer digging a cellar, on a promontory above the Ros' river, 98 m above sea level; excavated by Nadia Kruts and Ivan Pidoplichko, whose 1998 monograph remains the baseline) preserves four oval huts of 12–24 m², three contemporaneous, buried under 2.7–3.4 m of loess: the remains of at least ninety-five mammoths went into them — some twenty tonnes of bone per hut, roughly 900 kg per square meter of floor. The engineering is specific: a foundation ring of inverted, interlocking jawbones; roof arches of three dozen tusks still in their skull sockets, segments joined by carved ivory sleeves; a porch guarded by a painted mammoth-skull "drum" (red-ochre dots and lines, depressions on the cranial vault, two long-bone drumsticks with matching end damage); an engraved mammoth-rib plaque read since its discovery as a map of the settlement and its rivers; and amber ornaments plus fossil shells carried 350–500 kilometers — the site's portable wealth came from half a continent away. Sister dwellings stand at Mezin and as far west as Kraków's city center (three rings under the modern city, ~20 ka); Molodova V level 8 (~25 ka) is the oldest of the Ukrainian bone-art stations, and Gontsy, Pushkari, Kirillovskaya, and Dobranichevka complete the province [S — Pidoplichko 1998; A-grade site literature].

The province's deepest layer is not ours: Molodova's celebrated bone ring (~44,000 years ago, MIS 3) was built by **Neanderthals** — 116 selected bones (12 skulls, 5 mandibles, 14 tusks, 34 girdle bones, 51 long bones) from at least 15 mammoths, 25–26 hearths, forty thousand lithics, an internal domestic floor, and striations on dried scapulae that one excavator read as "calendars." Its interpretation has cycled through seven verdicts since 1931 — dwelling (Chernysh), hunting blind (Binford), natural accumulation (Klein), circular symbolic ring of Neanderthal belief (Stringer & Gamble), centrifugal living structure (Kolen), windbreak against periglacial wind (Hoffecker) — and the 2012 re-analysis by Demay, Péan, and Patou-Mathis, which found a deliberately built structure, a wooden superstructure or windscreen base, ochre-marked bones, and a domestic interior: the only attested Middle Palaeolithic mammoth-bone structure on Earth, in a wood-poor landscape, raised by people anticipating the cold [S]. Whether sapiens inherited the idea from Neanderthals is an open and beautiful question — the transmission would run through exactly the kind of contact the Caucasus file (Part Eleven) documents.

## 5.2 Africa

Africa's LGM refuge system was not peninsulas but **mosaics** — mountains, inselbergs, lakeshores, river systems — and its record survives in scattered museum drawers because colonial-era excavation and civil war dispersed the archives. Climate-envelope work suggests 27–66% of Africa remained human-refuge suitable through the LGM (Blinkhorn 2022), with the Congo rainforest contracting by roughly half and the Zambezi, Omo, and Great Rift lakes anchoring the water map — the mosaic had real boundaries.

### 5.2.1 The Ethiopian highlands — CONFIRMED

Sodicho rockshelter (1,930 m; excavated by the University of Cologne's CRC 806 "Out of Africa" project with Addis Ababa University) holds direct evidence for the classic refuge mechanism: occupation units bracketing the LGM and Heinrich events, dated 27–13.5 ka, showing people retreating *upslope* into a warm-temperate savanna refuge with residual water and game exactly when the Rift Valley lowlands (the Ziway-Shala basin) depopulated — the highlands wetter during the lowlands' arid phases, precisely as paleoclimate simulations predict [S — Hensel et al., *Frontiers in Earth Science* 2021]. And the twist: the highlands were abandoned during the *wetter* African Humid Period (~15–5 ka; sterile reddish paleosols), reoccupied around 4,750–4,360 years ago with the densest occupation of the whole sequence, and abandoned again within the last three centuries. Wetter was not better; the refuge logic ran on the region's own rules. The anthracology (2024) adds the management layer: hunter-gatherers maintained the open woodland-savanna ecotone with fire for twenty millennia, and the forest's return in the last thousand years tracks the *removal* of that human pressure — Africa's refuges were gardened, not found.

### 5.2.2 The Horn: inselberg refuges — CONFIRMED

At Guli Waabayo, on the Buur Heybe inselberg in inter-riverine Somalia, rock shelters trapped rain and nutrients in a 400–600 mm semi-desert — refugia inside refugia. The sequence runs 29,000–6,000 years in two layers: the Eibian industry through the arid MIS 2 (29–14.5 ka), then the Bardaale through the African Humid Period (14.5–6 ka), over 81,986 animal bones — the largest MIS 2/1 assemblage in East Africa. The subsistence is specific: dik-dik net-hunted by communal drives from at least 26,000 years ago (nets imply cooperation, territory, and delayed-return sociality), small game under 20 kg dominating throughout, occupation gaps tracking the arid pulses (23.1–19.7, 17.4–15.1, 12.6–11.2, 11.1–9.1 ka), and the first secure evidence that foragers mastered semi-arid East African lowlands during the LGM [S — Jones, *Archaeological and Anthropological Sciences* 2023, s12520-023-01894-2]. The history of the dig is the history of the region: Graziosi passed in 1935, J. Desmond Clark dug nearby in 1944, Steven Brandt's East African research program worked the shelters in the 1980s — and civil war froze the fieldwork. The Somali Academy's collections were exported in 1989 and now live in American museums (Washington University, then Florida; Wisconsin holds more): the archive of the Horn's ice age is physically a refugee.

### 5.2.3 The East African rock-shelter chain

Kisese II in Tanzania (Kondoa, in the UNESCO Kondoa Rock-Art Sites landscape; a six-meter sequence spanning 47,000–4,000 years) preserves the whole transition: ostrich-eggshell beads by 46–43 ka (possible blanks a layer older), ochre throughout, Levallois gone by 35.8–33.7 ka, bladelets by 22–18 ka, and backed microliths rare until they become *abundant* at 23.7–23.1 ka — exactly the LGM's onset, a technological answer to the squeeze, in a sequence excavated by the Leakeys (1935, 1951) and Raymond Inskeep (1956) whose field notes were **stolen from Inskeep's home in the 1990s**, nearly erasing the site from scholarship until reconstruction from the widow Adi's donated papers and the un-numbered collections of the National Museum in Dar es Salaam [S — Tryon, Ranhorn et al., *PLoS ONE* 2018, 10.1371/journal.pone.0192029; geoarchaeological confirmation of in-situ Pleistocene layers, Patania et al. 2022]. The Late Pleistocene fauna holds the southernmost records of Grevy's zebra and the extinct alcelaphine Damaliscus hypsodon; the burial ground (Laird et al. 2021) held at least six individuals, one dated to 7.2 ka, of unusually high morphological diversity — the adult long bones lost after excavation. Lukenya Hill, Enkapune ya Muto (blade tools by 46–50 ka — as old as the Levantine Upper Palaeolithic — and ostrich-eggshell beads at 40 ka: personal ornaments as alliance tokens, in Stanley Ambrose's reading), Mumba (1,780 beads of uniform 6.7 mm, a standardization that argues for exchange tokens; obsidian carried 320 km from Naivasha), and Nasera (OSL 36.8±3.4 ka) complete a chain showing East African forager networks running tens of millennia *before* the LGM and straight through it.

### 5.2.4 The Cape: the Mediterranean-climate larder

The Greater Cape Floristic Region — stable for millions of years (the fynbos biome's own deep history is itself a refugium debate, between long-stability and recent-instability schools), geophyte-rich, carbohydrate-dense, backed by a shoreline — "contributed to the survival of modern humans for at least 100,000 years," in the careful phrasing of Botha, Brenner and colleagues' foodplant fitness-landscape study, which modeled return rates across the Cape and tested them against the Klasies River Main Site record [S — *Journal of Archaeological Science* 2019, cited 24; the human land-use signature is now detected even in the genetics of a fynbos shrub, Leucadendron salignum — Pleistocene-impacting, catastrophically stressed in the last 300 years]. It is the other pole of the African refuge system: not rainforest-river refugia like the Horn, but a Mediterranean-climate pantry buffering the continent's southern end — the deep backdrop to Blombos (Part Nine) and the Later Stone Age tradition.

### 5.2.5 The archaic-retention complex — CONFIRMED

West Africa's record closes with the strangest file in this document. The Iwo Eleru skull (Nigeria, ~12–16 ka by U-series) is morphology *outside* modern human variation — robust, archaic-featured, nearest to late archaic Africans in multivariate shape space (Harvati et al., *PLoS ONE* 2011) — and it is not alone: the Ishang fossils and the Shum Laka genomes form one coherent picture. Shum Laka (Cameroon; four children, 8,000 and 3,000 years old, excavated by Pierre de Maret's team and sequenced by the Reich lab with the Ribot group) yielded the deepest known Y-chromosome lineage, **A00**, whose split estimate is ~31,000 years ago (CI 37–25 ka) — a dead-end branch proving A00 was once more diverse: West Cameroon, a long-term A00 refugium. Its autosomal ancestry is ~35% west-central-hunter-gatherer and ~65% a previously unknown basal West African lineage; the paper sketches a quadruple deep radiation 250–200 ka (central-African, southern-African, eastern-African hunter-gatherers, and a ghost) and a second radiation 80–60 ka that includes the non-African lineage; and it explicitly links its findings to Iwo Eleru's archaic-featured burial as concordant material evidence [S — Lipson et al., *Nature* 577:665, 2020, PMC8386425]. An 18,000-year-old East/South-Central African genomic study (six new individuals; the Skoglund group) shows three-source ancestry structure — eastern, southern, and a ubiquitous central-rainforest-hunter-gatherer-related component — stable from 80,000 to 20,000 years ago with remarkably little long-range gene flow [S — *Nature* 2022].

The genetics and the skulls now agree: **archaic-rich relict populations survived the terminal Pleistocene in West African refugia**, and the Mende's ~25,000-year-old replacement pulse (Part Two) shows the ice age stirring even Africa's deepest structure. What the complex *means* — isolation, hybrid persistence, or simply deep structure surfacing in morphology — is the same open question the Lagoa Santa and Kow Swamp files pose on other continents (Parts Ten and Twelve), and honesty requires leaving it open here too.

## 5.3 Asia

### 5.3.1 The Caucasus vault — CONFIRMED (with one giant caveat)

The southern Caucasus sheltered a genetically crucial population through the LGM. At Dzudzuana, in Georgia's Imereti region, the sequence runs from 34.5 ka to the terminal Paleolithic in four units; its Unit C — 27–24 kyr, the LGM proper — produced two genomes still available only as a 2018 preprint [P — Lazaridis et al., bioRxiv 423079; eight years influential, never peer-reviewed; treat with care, flagged wherever used]. The claim: Dzudzuana's people sat in a clade with the 8,000-year-old Anatolian early farmers (a clade that cannot be statistically rejected), closer to them than to the 13–10 ka Caucasus hunter-gatherers of the *same caves* — a "Basal Eurasian"-bearing population (the earliest appearance of the lineage that later saturates Near Eastern farmers) composed of ~72% Villabruna-related plus that basal component, with fewer shared alleles with Ust'-Ishim and Tianyuan than later Near Easterners have. The sweep assigned to them: Věstonice 16 at 35.7±11.3%, El Mirón at 60.6±11.3%, the later CHG and Iran_N at 45–64% Dzudzuana-like, the Karelia hunter-gatherers at 34±2.8% Villabruna-related plus ANE — and, in the preprint's largest sentence, the Dzudzuana population "contributed the majority of the ancestry of post-Ice Age people in the Near East, North Africa, and even parts of Europe, thereby becoming the largest single contributor of ancestry of all present-day West Eurasians." Until the paper publishes or fails, that sentence is the most-cited ghost in paleogenomics.

The same shelter preserved something equally astonishing: **dyed flax textiles**. Kvavadze and colleagues recovered 488 flax fibers in the pre-LGM Unit D (thirteen spun, fifty-eight dyed turquoise-to-black, several with cut marks), and from the LGM Unit C itself — the layer of the genomes — 787 fibers, eighteen spun, one **knotted**, thirty-eight dyed in black, gray, turquoise, and a single **pink**: a thread-and-dye tradition inside the refuge at the freeze's deepest point, twenty-six thousand years before agriculture [S — Kvavadze et al., *Science* 325:1359, 2009]. Whatever else the ice age took from the Caucasus, it left the oldest evidence that humans made colored thread.

Later Caucasus hunter-gatherers anchor the named component: **Satsurblia** (13.3 ka; Y J1-FT34521, mitochondrial K3) and **Kotias** (9.7 ka; Y J2-Y12379*, mitochondrial H13c). Their CHG component formed by the LGM seal — split from western lineages ~25 ka, ~45 ka from the early-farmer ancestors (Jones et al. 2015) — and models today run ~72% West Asian Upper Palaeolithic + 18% Caucasus UP + 10% eastern hunter-gatherer (Allentoft 2024, via Chataigner's synthesis), against the alternative reading (Vallini 2024) of 72% WEC2 plus 10% East Eurasian plus 18% Basal Eurasian. Caucasus mitochondrial diversity exploded at the glacial end ~18 ka [S — Margaryan et al. 2017]. The exports ran north to the steppe (Yamnaya roughly half CHG; Maykop 28–42% Hajji Firuz-related) and south to the Zagros and the Indian subcontinent (the Nalchik individual ties Volga herders to Zagros farmers). Modern Georgians retain ~47% CHG ancestry — the highest ice-age hunter-gatherer retention measured anywhere — and the Caucasus, in the 2019 genomic study's phrase, "acted as a bridge rather than a barrier": the LGM sealed it into a vault, and the vault seeded a hemisphere.

### 5.3.2 The Zagros and the Levant rim

Iran's Zagros mountains hold a deep Upper Palaeolithic: the **Baradostian** (the "Zagros Aurignacian," ~39–30 ka; bladelet-dominated, with ornaments — marine shell beads, perforated deer teeth, hematite pendants — in Shidrang's analyses; base camps like Yafteh, 29–39.5 ka on Bayesian models, anchoring the UNESCO-listed Khorramabad Valley cluster of Yafteh, Ghamari, Kunji, Gilvaran, and Gar Arjeneh; collections split between the National Museum of Iran and Yale) — and, decisively, a Neolithic genome: **WC1 of Wezmeh Cave** (~7,455–7,082 cal BCE, 10x coverage, already eating a farmer's diet by isotopes). WC1's runs of homozygosity are the shortest of his era: he descended from a **large hunter-gatherer population that never passed through an LGM bottleneck** [S — Broushaki et al., *Science* 353:499, 2016, PMC5113750, cited 467; the Zagros Neolithic cluster separated from the Anatolian one 46–77 ka, is closest to modern Pakistanis and Afghans — especially Iranian Zoroastrians — and is the main ancestor of modern South Asians; the Ganj Dareh individual GD13a, 1.39x, mitochondrial X2, matches it; Burger's "Federal Neolithic Core Zone" framing]. Europe's farmers-to-be bottlenecked through the freeze; the east's did not. Their neighbors, the Neanderthals of Shanidar, held the same mountains until ~45 ka (Part Eleven). At the rift's lowest point, Lake Lisan drowned and drained the Jordan valley floor (Part Six); on its receding shore, the Ohalo II fishing camp preserved the LGM's lakeside lifeway in waterlogged detail (Part Nine).

### 5.3.3 Tibet: the island in the sky

The Tibetan Plateau is the highest landscape humans colonized in the ice age, and for most of it the tenants were Denisovans: at **Baishiya Karst Cave** (Ganjia Basin, 3,280 m), a Denisovan mandible dated to 160,000 years ago (identified by ancient proteins — no DNA survives there), sedaDNA at 100–60 ka, and, from the 48,000–32,000-year layer, a Denisovan rib among butchered bharal blue sheep, wild yak, equids, **woolly rhino**, spotted hyena, marmots, and birds, with marrow extraction and bone tools: Denisovans hunted the high plateau through two glacial periods and the interglacial between, in a "relatively stable" basin refuge, and the timing and cause of their disappearance is an open question [S — Chen et al., *Nature* 2019; Zhang et al. 2024].

Modern humans first appear with blades at **Nwya Devu** as early as 45.6±2.6 ka (optically stimulated luminescence; the 2018 *Science* report gave 40–30 ka for 3,683 artifacts of black slate, with three occupation phases; Ge et al. 2024 pushed the blade assemblage older and added a lower-shore microblade complex at 10.3 ka) — "genuine Upper Palaeolithic blade technology, a possible expansion of H. sapiens," matching assemblages in Mongolia and Xinjiang rather than eastern China ("we don't know who these people were," Mark Aldenderfer; "my name is on late-peopling publications... I never really liked it," John Olsen). Then the **LGM gap**: not a single firmly dated plateau site, a true absence or a visibility artifact no one can yet say; the Younger Dryas shows reduced activity; permanent settlement arrives only around 13 ka at Chusang — whose claimed 169–226 ka footprint dates are contested [C — Zhang et al. 2021 *Science Bulletin* claim; Aubert & Pike's 2024 dating-recommendations paper names Qiusang as the "most recent precipitous claim," and a ~20 ka thermoluminescence date for the prints circulates unverified]. The high-altitude adaptation gene **EPAS1** arrived with Denisovan admixture in lowland East Asians 48–46 thousand years ago, long before anyone lived permanently on the plateau: the refugees inherited the mountain from the dead, and only barley (from ~5.2 ka) let them keep it.

### 5.3.4 East Asia: a continent of river-valley refuges

The genomic record of LGM East Asia resolves into a northern and a southern system [S — *Genome Biology* 2025 review, "160,000 years of East Asian genomes"; PMC12690904]. North: the Tianyuan individual (40 ka, near Beijing) and AR33K (33.6 ka) anchor a basal northeastern lineage with **excess Denisovan ancestry** and a surprising bridge westward — Tianyuan shares affinity with the 35,000-year-old GoyetQ116-1 of Belgium and with present-day Suruí of the Amazon: a genetic shadow of the pre-LGM north Eurasian population that would contribute to the first Americans (the Mal'ta boy at 24 ka sits close to this complex). The faint Australo-Melanesian signal in some Amazonian groups (strongest in the Suruí) has been hypothesized to ride a later Pacific-rim entry via the Aleutians ~9 ka [C — Pinotti 2019; thin but genomic]. After the LGM, the Amur River basin individual **AR19K** records a *different* northern population — a major turnover at the Siberia–East Asia crossroads — while northern China shows genetic continuity from ~14 ka (Mao et al.), and unpublished Houtaomuga genomes contribute significantly to Native American ancestry.

South: the 14,000-year-old **Red Deer Cave genome** (MZR, Longlin county) is an early-diverging southern lineage, distinct from the Hòabìnhian, that also contributed to Native Americans' gene pool; Qihe (11.7 ka, Fujian) already resembles Fujian Neolithic people; Longlin (10.7 ka) is morphologically archaic-looking yet genetically inside modern East Asian variation — retention under isolation, the same pattern as Iwo Eleru and Lagoa Santa. The **two-layer hypothesis** frames the Neolithic transformation: Hòabìnhian-related first layer, farmer second. Deep archaic anchors: Baishiya (above), the Harbin cranium (146 ka; Denisovan mitochondrial and proteomic signals), and three reconstructed introgression waves into Chinese genomes — one Neanderthal, two Denisovan, one of them shared with Oceanians and one East-Asian-specific.

The tool traditions point to a northeastern refuge complex: pressure microblade knapping appears earliest in **Hokkaido, 27,000–25,000 years ago** (Izuho's synthesis), with conceptual roots in the pre-LGM small-blade technocomplexes of southern Siberia, northern China, and Korea (the Bae/Yi school argues a southern Far East origin 30–25 ka; the Derevianko–Keates school pushes southern Siberia to 40–35 ka; Gómez Coutouly's techno-typological work and Graf's experiments sit between). The paleo-Sakhalin–Hokkaido–Kuril (PSHK) peninsula and the Sea of Japan coast are the hypothesized LGM refuge of the eastern lineage that later peopled the Americas (Graf & Buvit's synthesis; "conditions much more conducive than inland"), with the Mal'ta-related introgression into East Asians 24–20 ka hypothesized to occur somewhere between PSHK and Baikal, coincident with the microblade dispersal north. In the Amur basin, flat-bottomed pottery at Gasya and Khummi (16,200–14,100 years old) marks the LGM's end; in Jiangxi and Hunan, mobile foragers fired vessels twenty thousand years ago (Part Nine); in Japan, the Jōmon sequence runs from ~38 ka with the world's oldest ceramic culture (Part Nine's Ōdai Yamamoto I) and a basal East Eurasian genome at Ikawazu (2.5 ka, 27.5x; ~41% Ikawazu-related ancestry survives in Taiwan's Ami, ~8% in mainland Japanese, ~4% at Devil's Gate; coastal affinities only — Ami, Atayal, Ulchi, Nivkh — and **no Mal'ta/ANE affinity anywhere in East Asians, Jōmon included**, which breaks the assumption that the microblade spread was demic from Baikal).

### 5.3.5 Korea's unknown refuge — and the unsequenced peninsula

A 2026 *Quaternary Research* paper on **Chongphadae Cave** records human fossils, fireplaces, diverse ungulate fauna, and a flake-tool industry in layers 12–13, late MIS 3 into MIS 2, concluding — in the authors' own words — that "the central Korean Peninsula may have existed as an unknown refugium (or area of endemism) in northeastern Asia during this time" [S — *Quaternary Research*, Cambridge, 2026]. The frame agrees: LGM climate models put the peninsula at −5.85°C but only −14% precipitation — humid cold, versus the dry interiors — and temperate plants persisted in humid inland and northern pockets (Korean pine refugia confirmed at Changbai and in the peninsula itself, plus Xiaoxingan and, unexpectedly, Greater Khingan microrefugia in Populus koreana, bottleneck 21.25 ka, expansion 6.02 ka; the Baekdudaegan-spine debate between Chung's and Koo et al.'s camps runs unresolved). The human evidence exists and the botany agrees. What does not exist is a single ancient Korean genome from the Paleolithic: the first Korean genomes are 1,700-year-old Gaya-period individuals (*Current Biology* 2022), ironically **more** diverse than modern Koreans, with two individuals shifted toward Japanese/Jōmon ancestry — a possible signature of the indigenous hunter-gatherer substrate absorbed by millet and then rice farmers [S; the Jōmon-shifted interpretation remains P-grade speculation, flagged]. The "unknown refugium" is documented archaeologically and botanically, genetically invisible — the emptiest hole in East Asia's record.

### 5.3.6 Arabia: the pump, at rest

At the LGM Arabia returned to desert, its lakes and grasslands gone, its people — who had left tools at Khall Amayshan's lakes from 400 to 55 thousand years earlier — withdrawn to coastal refugia (the drowned Gulf basin below) or died out locally; the next chapter of Arabia is the Neolithic, after the rains return. The pump rested. One thread survives the quiet: the Arabian Bifacial Tradition and the fisher-hunter camps of the Gulf's shrinking shoreline, which the next ten thousand years would turn into the Ubaid and the world's first cities (Part Six's Gulf Oasis).

### 5.3.7 South Asia: the rainforest refuge

South Asia's ice-age record runs through its wet-zone refuges. At **Fa-Hien Lena**, Sri Lanka — the oldest known sapiens site in the region — microlithic technology appears 48,000–45,000 years ago alongside the earliest bow-and-arrow equipment outside Africa (bone points with impact fractures; Langley et al. 2020), fiber technology for nets and clothing, freshwater fishing gear, and shell beads traded from a coast over the horizon: a complete tropical forager adaptation, maintained through the LGM with none of Europe's boom-bust amplitude [S — Wedage et al., *PLoS ONE* 2019]. The Batadomba-lena and Kitulgala Beli-lena sequences (31–26 ka) carry the same tradition through the freeze; the ~30,000-year-old Fa Hien child and the Balangoda skeletons anchor the region's deep morphology, and the Vedda-relationship debate runs on sixteen millennia of claimed continuity [S — Kennedy & Deraniyagala]. In central India, Mehtakheri (~45 ka) and Patne (~25 ka) extend the record; beneath the Toba ash at Jwalapuram, occupation never stopped (Part Two). The verdict: the subcontinent's rain-shadowed refuges buffered climate swings too small to force extinction — the anti-Europe pattern, and the reason South Asia's later genomes descend from hunter-gatherer populations that never passed through a bottleneck at all [S — Broushaki et al. 2016]. The aDNA void remains absolute: not a single pre-Neolithic South Asian genome exists.

## 5.4 The Americas: Beringia and the double coast

**Beringia** — the unglaciated land bridge up to 1,000 km north-to-south joining Siberia to Alaska, the largest continuous high-latitude landmass of the glacial world — was first proposed as a glacial refuge by botanist Eric Hultén in the 1930s, and pollen confirms his reconstruction: a mesic tundra-steppe of grasses, sagebrush, and stands of spruce, birch, and alder — firewood, shelter, and game, including the ice age's last mammoths [A — historical]. The question is when, and how long, people held it.

- The **Beringian standstill** model comes in two chronologies. The mitochondrial-DNA school (Tamm et al. 2007, cited 939 times, with the long-chronology case built by Mulligan, Kitchen, and Miyamoto; 623 complete mtDNAs) argued for a long pause of up to 15,000 years, with the founder subclades of all Native Americans — A2, B2, C1b, C1c, C1d*, C1d1, D1, D4h3a, plus the minor X2a, D2, D3 — arising *in situ*, and only a handful of founders crossing; the model also caught a reverse flow — A2a lineages back-migrating into western Siberia's Evenks and Selkups, the standstill's echo in living people. The ancient-genome school (Raghavan et al. 2015, with the Mal'ta and Anzick genomes in hand) compressed it: divergence from Asians 23–20 ka, isolation under 10,000 years, a single migration 14–16 ka before Clovis. The reconciliation space is real: Graf and Buvit's archaeological synthesis warns that northeast Siberia itself was largely *abandoned* 30–16 ka (the Enisei and Transbaikal gaps run 24.8–22.7 ka even after date-cleaning), which cuts against a 15,000-year standstill but fits a shorter one [C — between schools].
- **Yana River** (northeast Siberia, 34,500–31,500 years ago; IntCal20): a cluster of open-air sites near the paleo-Yana mouth, thousands of artifacts (tools of bone, antler, and ivory, including the earliest known eyed needle), fauna from steppe bison to mammoth, probable winter occupation on the southern upland margin — and two human teeth whose genomes define the **Ancient North Siberians**: a population of perhaps 500 effective people, recently arrived from the west, remarkably *not* inbred (contrast Chagyrskaya's Neanderthals), carrying the ancestral FADS1 allele suited to an animal-fat diet and lacking the vitamin-D-pathway adaptations of later polar peoples [S — Hoffecker et al., *Proceedings of the Royal Society B* 2023, PMC9832545; Sikora et al. 2019; the "GAP" — the glacial-age plain of the exposed East Siberian Arctic Shelf — was their hunting world, gone under the sea].
- **The biomarker fight**: at Burial Lake and Lake E-5 in the Brooks Range, fecal-sterol ratios (coprostanol to stigmastanol above 0.18 = human input) spike at 24,000–23,000 years ago and polycyclic-aromatic-hydrocarbon fluxes — fire, when lightning ignition was suppressed — run elevated through 34–16 ka: argued as evidence of human presence through the LGM [C — Vachula 2019/2020]. A 2022 field check of E-5 by Ted Goebel's team found no archaeology at all, in any landform: either the signal is microbial noise (sterols degrade), or visibility is impossibly low. Hoffecker's verdict: biomarkers plus Bluefish plus the genetics make the standstill "a reasonable inference"; the artifact record alone cannot yet carry it. Open.
- **Bluefish Caves (Yukon)**: the old collections hold fifteen bones with confident cut marks (ten in Cave I, five in Cave II — horse mandible with tongue removed at 19,650±130 14C BP; caribou pelvis filleted at 18,570±110; wapiti premaxilla skinned) and twenty more probable, with micromorphology (breadth ratios 12–18 μm, depths 91–95 μm, opening angles 139–144°) matching stone tools and excluding carnivores; six new AMS dates span 10,490–19,650 14C BP = 12,000–24,000 cal BP [C — Bourgeon, Burke & Cinq-Mars, *PLoS ONE* 2017; the "caves as short-term horse-hunting camps" reading, spring-summer kills by cementochronology, against the Tanana valley's residential open-air sites; the 31–42 ka claims circulating online are NOT in the peer-reviewed chain]. Since 2019 the **Odyssey program** (Kansas Geological Survey, Rolfe Mandel and Dennis O'Rourke, with the Vuntut Gwitch'in First Nation) has tested directly: Cave III (2019) yielded ~150 bones and no cut marks; newly discovered **Cave IV**, sealed in loess, is the live hope — eight stones and 161 faunal pieces, no cultural modifications yet, seventeen bones in the UC-Irvine lab as of the 2024 season. Gwich'in heritage researcher Brandon Kyikavichik endorses the butchery-camp reading from oral tradition. The old claims stand unretracted; the new fieldwork is so far null but pointed.
- South of the ice, North America's LGM refuges were the **Southeast** (Appalachians, Gulf and Atlantic coastal plains — the temperate refugium where bison and black bears also survived in southern pockets) and the **Pacific coast**, where kelp forests — sustained by 8–11°C seas — formed a continuous, boat-friendly food corridor from Japan to Chile: the **kelp highway** [S-adjacent — Erlandson et al. 2007 and successors; the evidence chain runs from Monte Verde's kelp species and Port Eliza's 16-ka-viable sediments to Cedros Island fishhooks (>11.5 ka), Channel Islands sites, Vancouver Island goats (12 ka), and Prince of Wales bears (12.5 ka)]. The interior ice-free corridor between the Laurentide and Cordilleran sheets was closed from ~26,000 to ~14,000 years ago; when it opened (Lesnek's deglaciation work: viable by ~17 ka, productive later), it opened onto depauperate ground, and the microblade-Yubetsu toolkit that walked into Alaska ~14 ka would be replaced south of the ice by Clovis bifaces — a technological extinction in mid-migration [S — Graf's synthesis]. Every route into the continents ran through refuges of one kind or another.

## 5.5 Sahul: the southern continent's coastal ladder

Australia was peopled 60,000–65,000 years ago, and the long chronology now wins a definitive genomic test: 2,456 mitogenomes — the largest dataset ever assembled for the region — resolve into a **northern route** (Borneo→Sulawesi→Halmahera/Seram/Aru→the Bird's Head of New Guinea: haplogroups M29′Q, P, M28, M27, M25; ~100% of New Guinea/Bismarck/Solomons founders and ~36% of Australian lineages) and a **southern route** (the Lesser Sundas — Flores/Timor→northwest Australia: S, O, M42a/c, R12, M14, M15/16, N13; ~64% of Australian lineages), with founder haplogroups dating M to 67–72 ka, N to 63–73 ka, R to 61–70 ka, a new M42′74 clade supporting the southern coastal exit out of Africa, crossings of up to ~100 km of open water, and simulations requiring founders in the hundreds moving deliberately and fast [S — *Science Advances*, November 2025, ady9493; the dual-route model corroborated on Papuan genomes by Brucato 2021, which favored the northern route; landfalls at the Bird's Head and the northwest shelf, earliest settlement on the Bird's Head, post-LGM back-migrations S2a2 (16–27 ka, Australia→New Guinea) and Q2a (15–21 ka)]. Y-chromosome concordance: S-B254's early branches in the Philippines and Aeta; C1b2 Sahul-wide. The ancestors of the P haplogroup passed through the Philippines — where basal P lineages in the Aeta may record encounters with *Homo luzonensis* — and Wallacean mtDNA sits almost entirely nested in Papuan clades, with back-flows ~15 ka and ~3 ka.

Within Sahul, the LGM's desertification — the interior's rainfall down ~60%, temperatures ~10°C below modern — squeezed people toward the coasts and the big river systems: the Gulf Plains (before the Carpentarian lake rose), the Murray–Darling, the Wet Tropics coastal strip, the Tasmanian highlands. The demographic clock is precise: Aboriginal Australians and Papuans split ~25,000–40,000 years ago [S — Malaspinas et al., *Nature* 538:207, 2016 — a single founding population, divergence from Eurasians 51–72 ka, Denisovan admixture ~4% at ~44 ka *before* the split, the split plausibly driven by the LGM's hyper-arid interior and the rise of the landlocked Carpentarian lake ~40 ka on the Arafura Sill], and the Pama-Nyungan language family's Holocene expansion reads in the genes as a thin film: "a few immigrants who changed the way people spoke and thought — just to disappear again like ghosts" [S — Willerslev]. The deepest sites anchor the antiquity: **Madjedbebe** in Arnhem Land (the 2017 *Nature* chronology of 59.3–65 ka, accepted in outline by most specialists — Michael Hiscock among the country's leading skeptics accepts ~60,000 years — with unresolved stratigraphic critiques: termite disturbance flagged by Wood, sediment-mixing arguments by Williams and colleagues; the Mirarr people's Jabiluka lease expired in 2024, the country to be absorbed into Kakadu) [C]; and **Willandra Lakes**, where Mungo Lady's cremation and Mungo Man's ochre burial (~40 ka) remain the oldest ritual funerals on Earth, WLH50's robust calvarium records the archaic-morphology retention theme (Part Ten), and 124 footprints on a Pleistocene soak — discovered in 2003 by Barkindji man Mary Pappin Jr. on survey — date to 19,000–23,000 years ago: the largest trackway collection of the ice age, left by tall, fast runners on the drying lake's last margin. The Willandra people were also grinding wild grass on stones before 18,000 years ago, contemporary with the Middle East's first grain experiments: the same subsistence logic, an ocean apart.

The Sahul founders crossed a drowned continent's worth of piers — Sahul itself (Australia + New Guinea + Tasmania) held until ~9 ka — and every pier is now underwater except the Australian mainland's edge and the highlands: the first Australians, like the first Japanese and the first Britons, are a coastal people whose oldest country is on the shelf.

## 5.6 The drowned Southeast: Sundaland and the island relicts

Beneath the South China Sea lies the ice age's largest lost world. At the LGM the Sunda Shelf stood exposed — Borneo, Java, Sumatra, and Malaya joined as **Sundaland**, roughly Europe-sized, drained by river systems mapped since Molengraaff's 1922 synthesis (the Malacca, Siam, and Sunda catchments, running to a shelf-edge delta). The drowning schedule is mapped in twenty-seven steps by the Field Museum's atlas: from −116 m at 21 ka to modern zero by ~6,070 years ago, then a mid-Holocene highstand of +5 m around 4.2 ka; Hanebuth's seismic work clocks the fastest rise at ~16 m per 300 years [S — Hanebuth et al., *Science* 288:1033, 2000; Sathiamurthy & Voris 2006]. The drowned record is finally surfacing: in 2025, dredged fill-sands from the Solo River's submerged lowstand valley in the Madura Strait — collected in 2015, dated by OSL to 162±31 and 119±27 ka (MIS 6) — yielded the first vertebrate fossils from subsea Sundaland: over six thousand specimens, at least thirty-six species (turtles, crocodiles, monitor lizards including *Varanus komodoensis*, pythons, sharks including the extinct *Hemipristis serra*, hippos, bovids, cervids, proboscideans, stegodons, macaques), and **two Homo erectus cranial fragments**: Madura Strait 1, a late Javanese erectus with a flat supratoral plane and weak postorbital constriction, closest to Sambungmacan; and MS2 — with grassland lowland species (Duboisia, Axis lydekkeri, Epileptobos) believed extinct by the end of the Middle Pleistocene persisting on the plains, high-crowned Elephas molars, small Bos palaeosondaicus [S — Berghuis et al., *Quaternary Environments and Humans* 3(2), 2025]. The find extends erectus far east of Java onto the drowned shelf — and the researchers' prediction is the sentence to remember: **Denisovan fossils may lurk in the submerged valleys.**

The islands above the drowning held archaic relicts until surprisingly late:

- **Borneo**: the Niah "Deep Skull" (~35–40 ka) — once read as an "Australo-Melanesian" adolescent male — is a gracile older woman with affinities to present-day Borneans [S — Curnoe et al. 2016], and a hominin tooth from nearby Gua Danang, from a layer dated ~54,000 years ago, may yet push an archaic human into Borneo proper [P — morphology pending].
- **Flores**: *Homo floresiensis*, descended from erectus-size ancestors at Mata Menge by ~700,000 years ago (Kaifu's 2024 dwarfing-within-300-kyr analysis), with a tool record back to 1.02 Ma at Wolo Sege ("probably accidental rafting," in the Liang Bua team's own careful words), occupied Liang Bua until 61,000–55,000 years ago — and a 2025 stalagmite record assigns their end to **climate aridification**: rainfall fell 37% between 76 and 61 ka, severe summer drought arrived by 61–55 ka, the endemic stegodons collapsed, the hobbits abandoned the cave, and a major volcanic eruption followed ~50 ka; modern humans appear at Liang Bua only ~46,000 years ago. The hobbits were already gone; sapiens inherited the stage. No floresiensis DNA survives in modern Flores pygmies [S — Gagan et al., *Communications Earth & Environment* 2025].
- **Luzon**: *Homo luzonensis* — seven teeth and six bones of at least three individuals (CCH1: a third metatarsal; the Callao Cave teeth and finger and toe bones), a mosaic of Australopithecus-like and modern features (Détroit et al., *Nature* 2019) — initially dated to 50–67 ka; Grün's 2023 uranium-series revision pushes the remains to **at least ~134,000 years ago** (134±14 ka; the original dates were young because of uranium uptake), and butchered rhino at Kalinga shows hominins on the island — never connected by land — 709,000 years ago. They crossed open sea, repeatedly, over a million years before us; locals call such beings *Ubag*.
- **Wallacea as Denisovan country**: the genomic stack shows at least three deeply divergent Denisovan groups feeding into the region's people: the D1 pulse into Sahul's ancestors ~46 ka, the D2 pulse ~25–31 ka after arrival, and the Philippines' independent D3 pulse reaching ~5% in the Ayta Magbukon — the world's highest Denisovan fractions, up to 46% above Papuans after masking (Larena et al., *Current Biology* 2021; Uppsala; Swedish Research Council and Knut & Alice Wallenberg funding; data behind an Indigenous-governed access committee, EGA EGAS00001005408); East Asians, separately, carry a more Altai-like pulse, D0 (Browning et al. 2018); Reilly et al. 2026 (*Science*, Tucci lab) find three Denisovan-like sources in Near Oceanians, with method heterogeneity honestly flagged. A 164,000–131,000-year-old Denisovan child's molar comes from Cobra Cave, Laos — tropical, DNA-less, identified by proteins and morphology (Demeter et al., *Nature Communications* 2022; the child was 3.5–8.5 years old, warm-adapted).
- **The Toalean foragers**: the 7,200-year-old woman of Leang Panninge — Bessé' — buried in Sulawesi's Maros highlands in fetal position under river cobbles, with galip-nut offering (14C 7.3–7.2 ka), red ochre, hunted-animal bone, and Maros points, her petrous bone yielding tropical DNA at a landmark success rate: ~50% basal East Asian + ~50% Australasian, 2.2% Denisovan, diverging *after* Denisovan admixture but from neither Australians nor Papuans — a previously unknown lineage with no detected descendants [S — Carlhoff et al., *Nature* 597:543, 2021; Brumm, Oktaviana, Posth, Krause]. The Toalean world covered only ~10,000 km² of the southwest peninsula — complex foragers at high density, warty-pig hunters who may have managed their prey, crossing 30 km of open water to Selayar — and its points have their own micro-taxonomy (Perston et al. 2021: four subclasses — classic Maros with hollow base and denticulations, Mallinrung, Lompoa, Pangkep — pressure-flaked with flake-edge indentors, hafting notches averaging 8.3 × 3.8 mm; the poison-edge speculation remains residue-untested). The culture vanished by the fifth century AD.
- **Hòabìnhian mainland**: the mainland's hunter-gatherer genome record is two individuals — Pha Faen, Laos (7,888±40 BP) and Gua Cha, Malaysia (4,319±64 BP) — from a lineage (McColl et al., *Science* 2018: 43 skeletons hunted across seven countries for 2.5 years; 26 genomes; the Hòabìnhian deeply diverged from main East Asian lineages, present Southeast Asians mixing it with farmers and others) that preceded and underlies modern Southeast Asians. The burial file is richer than the genome file: Gua Cha's fifteen flexed graves without offerings; the ~10,000-year-old Gua Gunung Runtuh burial with shells under and over the body, charred pig, deer, lizard, and monkey bones at the arm and shoulder, a sumatralith and tools as grave goods, and cranial affinities documented toward Gua Cha *and* Aboriginal Australians (Matsumura & Zuraina 1999) — Australo-Melanesian continuity in the peninsula, matching the First Settler retention pattern.

The deep lesson of the Southeast: **every sea gap sapiens crossed to reach Sahul had been crossed before, by other humans.** The archipelago was an archaic graveyard that became a sapiens highway — and its drowned floor almost certainly still holds the missing bones: the Madura Strait erectus fossils are the first proof of what the shelf preserves.

## 5.7 The Mediterranean islands: late doors

The Mediterranean was cold-steppe shoreline at the LGM — its islands close, but never connected. The oldest uncontested island crossings are post-glacial: Melos obsidian reaching Franchthi Cave in Greece by ~13,000 BC, "the earliest clear documentation of seafaring in the world" [S-adjacent — Perlès 1979; Laskaris 2011]; Cyprus's first mariners by ~12,000 BC, arriving into a pygmy-hippo world they may have helped finish: at the Aetokremnos rock shelter, 300,000-plus bones (98% the Cypriot pygmy hippo, *Phanourios minutus*) under a Younger Dryas-age layer preserve the fight between the hunting reading (intact strata, anthropogenic burning, a prime-dominated mortality profile that statistically matches culled assemblages — the Wopschall dissertation) and the natural-death reading (accidents, genetic abnormality, endemic disease; wild boar arrived by human ferry ~11.5 ka either way) [C — Simmons versus Ammerman and Nicolaou]; Sardinia's Corbeddu Cave preserving modern humans ~20,000 years ago, among the earliest Mediterranean sea travel (Tykot's reading), on an island that was probably the last of the large Mediterranean islands to be colonized (Sondaar's judgment).

The older claims — Crete's Plakias survey with handaxes in uplifted marine terraces dated ≥130 ka (Strasser & Panagopoulou; *Hesperia* 2011: 28 sites, 2,100+ lithics observed, 211 collected, 30+ bifaces; the logic: Crete has been an island for five million years, so the tools imply ≥40 km open-water crossings, and at the LGM lowstand still three sequential crossings from Turkey or Greece; Ferentinos' parallel work on the Ionian islands raises Neanderthal seafaring on 5–12 km hops; "the seas as a highway, not a barrier") [C — real tools, uncertain context, no confirming excavation since; the Tourloukis-and-Karkanas-type reviews put the earliest *uncontested* travel at 13 ka] — would put seafaring deep in the Middle Palaeolithic, but the confirming digs have never come.

Each island, once reached, became its own refuge and its own evolutionary laboratory — a pattern the ice age repeated everywhere from Flores to Cyprus to Sardinia, whose first mitochondrial genomes (Su Carroppu, ~10 ka: a dual Near-Eastern K1a2d and Western-European U5b1i1 origin for its first inhabitants, with the Y-chromosome side dominated by I2a1a1-M26 at ~39% today) still await their first Mesolithic nuclear genome [S — Modi et al., *Scientific Reports* 2017; Olivieri et al., *Molecular Biology and Evolution* 2017; for the nuclear era, Marcus et al. 2020, in Part Twelve]. Sicily, Malta (whose dwarf elephants and hippos were gone before humans arrived, a natural control), and the Aegean's drowned Paralia all complete the ring: a basin where the post-glacial sea wrote the settlement map — and then erased the first drafts.

## 6.1 The schedule of the flood

The ice did not melt politely. Sea-level reconstructions from coral reefs, deltas, and ice-sheet physics record **meltwater pulses** — bursts of global ocean rise measured in meters per decade [S — Deschamps et al., *Science* 2012 (Tahiti IODP-310 cores, cited 711); Teller, Leverington & Mann, *Quaternary Science Reviews* 2002; Lambeck 2014]:

- **MWP-1A0**: ~19,600–18,800 BP — at least 10 meters in 800 years, registered from Bonaparte Gulf to five other far-field sites. The post-LGM refugee era opens.
- **MWP-1A**: 14,650–14,310 BP — 12–22 meters in under 350 years (best estimate 14–18 m), peak rates above 40 mm per year; a short 10–15 m rise actually preceded 18 ka, then a stall, then the main event. **Source verdict**: sea-level fingerprinting assigns most of the water to the North American (Laurentide + Cordilleran) and Eurasian ice sheets — Lin et al.'s 2021 fingerprint puts 17.9 m in 500 years on those two alone; Brendryen et al. (*Nature Geoscience* 2020) add the Eurasian Ice Sheet's collapse at 4.5–7.9 m as "a major source"; Gregoire's 2012 saddle-collapse mechanism (the Cordilleran–Laurentide separation) is the switch that simultaneously *opens the ice-free corridor* into the continent's interior. Antarctica's contribution was small or late. This is the pulse that drowned Sundaland's shelf, drained Lake Lisan, and broke the Aegean lowlands.
- **MWP-1B**: 11,500–11,200 BP — downgraded and possibly illusory. The old Barbados claim of 28 meters (Fairbanks 1989) has collapsed; Tahiti cores detect no step above ~5 m at the method's limit (Bard 2009); Liu & Milliman's 13-m-in-300-years reading (−58 to −45 m) stands at one end, Stanford's 2011 synthesis at the other — a robust *enhanced interval* of 11.5–8.8 ka at ≤25 mm/yr, with Svalbard's near-field record showing 40–80 m of *falling* relative sea level from rebound at 11.3–11.0 ka. Honest presentations now say "perhaps 5–13 meters, possibly smeared" [C].
- **MWP-1C**: 8,200–7,600 BP — the Laurentide's Agassiz-Ojibway superlake disgorged ~163,000 km³ of freshwater in about a year through Hudson Strait, weakening Atlantic circulation and cooling the hemisphere (the **8.2 ka event**), while raising the sea again; the Storegga tsunami strikes at ~8,150 BP inside this window, and Herrle's Aegean record registers the second pulse at 7.6 ka (below).
- **The Younger Dryas interval**: deglacial sea-level rise *stalled* while the ice readvanced; a possible small Arctic trigger pulse at ~12.9 ka (Tarasov & Peltier) sits inside it.

Do the human arithmetic: at MWP-1A rates, a camp twenty meters above the beach loses its shoreline to a hundred-meter-wide new lagoon every couple of years. Sundaland lost roughly a third of its exposed land between 14,600 and 12,000 years ago. Britain became an island around 8,000 years ago, give or take the sill-breach debate. The ice age's refugees became the *water* age's refugees — often the same people, twice.

## 6.2 Doggerland: the lost heartland

The North Sea floor was dry land until roughly 7,000 years ago — a lowland named Doggerland (after the Dogger Bank, itself named for the seventeenth-century Dutch fishing boats, the *doggers*) by archaeologist Bryony Coles in the 1990s. The idea's prehistory is a parable of institutional blindness: Clement Reid proposed the drowned forests in his 1913 book *Submerged Forests* and was ignored for fifty years, until North Sea oil-and-gas seismic surveys proved the landscape; when the company PGS donated some 2,300 square miles of three-dimensional survey data, Vince Gaffney's team produced the first images of a river valley system (the Shotton River), and the North Sea Palaeolandscapes Project went on to map about 23,000 km² — "the largest well-preserved prehistoric archaeological landscape in the world," Europe's richest Mesolithic hunting ground, in Gaffney and Kenneth Thomson's judgment possibly supporting semi-permanent settlement [A — academic syntheses]. Finds now include the Colinda harpoon (1931: a barbed antler point dredged from 120 feet of water at the Leman and Ower Banks by the trawler *Colinda*'s skipper, Pilgrim Lockwood — for decades the lone proof people argued about), a Neanderthal skull fragment dredged at the Middeldiep off Zeeland in 2009 (Leiden), a flint flake with birch-bark-tar hafting — Neanderthal glue technology — dredged off the Dutch coast in 2019, a 13,000-year-old engraved aurochs bone from the Brown Bank, and — in 2019 — the first successfully prospected deep-sea archaeological site, a hammerstone forty kilometers offshore at thirty-two meters depth on the ancient Southern River estuary. Gaffney's working rule is humbling: there are no known settlement sites anywhere more than eight miles offshore, because nobody has properly looked — offshore wind-farm development is the next frontier, and the Europe's Lost Frontiers project (Bradford) is coring the seabed for sedimentary DNA.

The **Storegga tsunami** (~8,150 years ago) was long imagined as Doggerland's cinematic end: the largest known Holocene submarine landslide (80,000 km² of seabed off Norway; ~3,200 km³ of sediment displaced; deposits from Greenland to Denmark), raising run-ups of up to twenty meters in Shetland (thirty-five corrected for the era's lower sea level) and several meters on the Scottish mainland, arriving in autumn — fish bones and twigs in the Norwegian deposits date the season. The model war has three camps: Weninger's *catastrophic final flooding* (2008: the wave swept a Dogger Bank that was already a last island; hundreds to three thousand people in the danger zones; stored food destroyed; local bands possibly exterminated), Hill's numerical models (if the bank was exposed, maximum ~35% land inundation — "an extreme high tide," the energy dissipated across the shallows), and the Walker–Gaffney synthesis (2020: the first evidence from the *southern* North Sea — core ELF001A through the old Great Ouse valley — showing oak-hazel meadowland absorbing the wave, the surge guided down the palaeochannel, bear, boar, and ruminant eDNA in the layers, no human occupation horizon found, the tsunami causing no permanent sea-level rise at all, and the "Dogger Littoral" remnant plausibly surviving into the Neolithic, 1,150 years *after* the wave). Two confirmed Mesolithic sites under Storegga deposits exist, both near the source (Norway; Inverness). The popular media's "a quarter of Mesolithic Britain drowned in one night" is an unsourced extrapolation [M — flagged].

What is *not* disputed changed in March 2026: the largest sedimentary-DNA study ever conducted on a drowned landscape — 252 samples from 41 marine cores along a 30-km stretch of the Southern River, with rigorous separation of fine (local, secure) from coarse (transported, insecure) sediment — found **temperate oak-elm-hazel woodland established by 16,000 years ago**, when all of northwest Europe was tundra and ice stood at the Scottish border; *Tilia* 2,000 years earlier than any British record; ***Pterocarya*** — a walnut relative presumed absent from northwest Europe for 400,000 years — surviving in the southern river valleys; boar, deer, bear, and aurochs eDNA; and parts of the landscape staying above water until about 7,000 years ago [S — Allaby et al., *PNAS*, March 2026]. Doggerland was not a frigid doomed plain: it was a **northern refuge that kept temperate life alive through the freeze** — a microrefugium solving "Reid's Paradox" (how Britain recolonized so fast after the ice), and, as Allaby put it, possibly "why relatively little early Mesolithic evidence survives on mainland Britain." The catastrophe narrative must now be written against a forested, people-friendly Dogger Littoral persisting into the Neolithic.

The human story completes the circle: Britain was abandoned for over ten thousand years as the LGM closed in (Part Seven), recolonized from the continent around 15,000 years ago, and then severed again by the very sea that had fed it. No peer-reviewed ancient genome from Doggerland exists yet; the 8,300-year-old jaw with cut-marks — possible mortuary ritual, preserved by anoxic seabed mud — is the lead thread [A]. The flood myths found Doggerland anyway: Langdon's 2025 "Atlantis Found" fits Plato's dimensions to the Dogger Bank at R²=0.998 and reads Stonehenge's Slaughter Stone as a relief map — a specimen of how real discoveries get mythologized, dissected in Part Ten.

## 6.3 Sundaland: the schedule and the fossils

The drowning of Sundaland is the best-mapped large-scale homeland loss in prehistory: the Field Museum's atlas reconstructs the transgression in 27 steps — from −116 m at 21 ka to modern zero by ~6,070 years ago, then a mid-Holocene highstand of +5 m around 4.2 ka — on foundations laid by Molengraaff's 1922 mapping of the Sunda river systems, with Hanebuth's seismic work clocking the fastest rise at ~16 m per 300 years: the sea eating a Europe-sized land in centuries, its coastal-swamp forests relocating hundreds of kilometers in a few hundred years — the fastest large-scale ecosystem migration on record [S — Sathiamurthy & Voris 2006; Hanebuth et al., *Science* 288:1033, 2000; the Irwanto "Atlantis of the Java Sea" genre maps land-loss snapshots from 15/11.6/8/6 ka onto this real schedule — the myth machinery feeding one cycle behind the data, Part Ten's deconstruction axiom].

What the sea took, it is now returning: the Madura Strait erectus fossils and grassland fauna (Part Five); and even the living megafauna carry the refugium's signature — Borneo's elephants have been isolated ~300,000 years, cut off by a sea-level rise, a refugium written in mitochondrial DNA [S — Fernando 2003]. The drowning also preserved what dry land could not: waterlogged sites like Ohalo's kin must lie by the thousand on the shelf — the reason Southeast Asia's early record reads thin is that its richest chapters are under forty meters of the South China Sea.

## 6.4 The Black Sea and the Caspian cascade: three floods that weren't (quite) Noah's

The Black Sea basin holds the most myth-charged water in archaeology. The cast of hypotheses, in order of appearance and shrinking drama:

- **Ryan–Pitman flood hypothesis** (1997 *Marine Geology* 138:119; revised 2003): the Mediterranean burst through the Bosporus ~8,400 years ago, raising the freshwater Neoeuxinian lake by 60–70 meters, flooding 70,000–100,000 km² in a cataract of ~50 km³ per day, rising fifteen centimeters a day for at least three hundred days, and — in the authors' telling — displacing Neolithic farmers into Europe and seeding the flood memories of Mesopotamia [C — historically pivotal]. The origin story is a standing caution: William Ryan and Walter Pitman began from the suspicion that flood myths had a geological basis, sought the geology to fit, and saw their original paper accepted for publication one day after receipt — a fast track critics have never let the field forget.
- **The Outflow Hypothesis** (Aksu, Hiscott, Mudie, and colleagues, from 2002's *GSA Today* through the 2022 synthesis): the Black Sea flowed *out* into the Marmara and Aegean from ~11.5–11.1 ka, *before* any saline inflow; the supposed flood horizon is a climbing delta at the Bosporus exit; Mediterranean freshening, Aegean sapropels, and seven of Ryan and Pitman's key observations are all reinterpreted without catastrophe [S-adjacent — the majority reading]. Giosan et al.'s cap: any Holocene rise was 5–10 m over ~2,000 km², not 100,000 km².
- **The Chepalyga Late Pleistocene flood** — the serious catastrophe is *older and comes from the Caspian*. During the deglaciation, meltwater and permafrost melt filled the Caspian basin until it overtopped the Manych–Kerch sill **17,000–14,000 years ago**, discharging on the order of 50,000 m³ per second (peak reconstructions exceed 60,000) into the Black Sea and raising it tens of meters — one node in an "Epoch of Extreme Inundations" that linked Baltic, Caspian, and Black spillways into a 3,000-km cascade of giant water bodies (at peak: ~1.5 million km² of water, 700,000 km³ of it, five thousand cubic kilometers of salt) [C between Chepalyga's maximalist reading and the revised chronologies: Kurbanov's OSL program dates the Early Khvalynian Chocolate Clays to 17–13.1 ka, and Butuzova's Kosika section shows the Caspian near *modern* level already by 23–22 ka (Badyukova's camp disputes the deep-regression reading); Dolukhanov's 2010 synthesis brackets the main transgressions at 13.6–11.8 ka; Svitoch rejects both the Siberian-overflow trigger and the "Noachian prototype" framing]. Yanchilina's work shows Heinrich Stadial 1 meltwater flooding *both* basins at once, and the Manych-floor erosion qualifies as a genuine megaflood.

The archaeology of the cascade is the honest core: the Middle Palaeolithic site of Kamennaya Balka sat isolated by the spillway's activity, its middle layer (17–15 ka) an indigenous culture cut off from Near Eastern connections while the water ran; Avdeevo, Byki, and Kapova felt the same compression — Chepalyga's reading is "reduction of living space, mass migration, population-density increase, stimulus to farming"; Dolukhanov's synthesis treats the spillway as a **cultural barrier for 5,000 years**, with Upper Palaeolithic small-tool connections crossing only after the crest at 12.5–12 ka. A persistent minority hypothesis even proposes Neanderthal survival in the Caspian basin through the LGM regression [C — flagged, unfalsified]. And the archaeology verdict on the *Holocene* flood stands from multiple specialists (Yanko-Hombach's review; Anthony; Dolukhanov & Shilik; Stanko): **no population-dynamic catastrophe is detectable at 14–6 ka in the Pontic record** — "small audience and small effects" (Anthony). The UNESCO-IUGS IGCP 521 project (2005–2009) was funded precisely to test all of this.

The honest position: the Holocene Black Sea "deluge" was probably not the Flood's source — but the Late Pleistocene *Caspian cascade*, 17,000–14,000 years ago, squarely inside our ice-age window, was a flood of truly biblical discharge that reshuffled whole populations of the western steppe. Its memory plausibly echoes in the region's later water mythology — routed through the Gulf Oasis literature (below) into the Mesopotamian corpus of Part Twelve. And one confound belongs in the record: the Agassiz-Ojibway outburst (163,000 km³ into the Labrador Sea) sits within centuries of Ryan and Pitman's revised flood date — global hydroclimate was doing dramatic things at 8.4 ka with or without any Bosporus breach.

## 6.5 The Gulf Oasis: the testable Atlantis

Jeffrey Rose's *Current Anthropology* paper (2010, cited 225 times; University of Birmingham) proposes that the floor of the Persian Gulf — exposed from ~75,000 years ago, drained by the Tigris, Euphrates, Karun, and Wadi Batin and fed by subterranean karst aquifers, reaching Great-Britain size *precisely when the surrounding land was driest* (the inverse relationship is the hypothesis's elegant core: maximum oasis at maximum glacial aridity) — was a refugium and crossroads on the road out of Africa. His test logic: more than sixty well-developed coastal sites appear "virtually overnight" around 7,500 years ago — stone houses, long-distance trade, decorated pottery, domesticates, boats — with no precursors on land, implying precursors now drowned; and Jebel Faya's three Paleolithic settlement phases (125–25 ka) sit just outside the basin's perimeter [C — "hypothesis," by its author's own careful framing; supportive comments from Carter ("bold and imaginative... would completely rewrite the out-of-Africa story") and Černý ("excellent theory"); the ironclad test — Paleolithic stone tools recovered from the drowned floor — has never been run: no marine-Paleolithic program exists in the Gulf as of 2026, the INA's Bahrain surveys having targeted shipwrecks and the Iranian GPR programs historical ports]. The regional sequence records the aftermath either way: an occupational "Dark Millennium" ~5.9 ka, then reoccupation and the Ubaid-to-Jemdet-Nasr coastal networks — Dilmun, Magan — from which Sumerian civilization grew [A].

If the Gulf ever returns its secrets, it completes the most important triangle in this document: Doggerland (drowned, no flood text — its people's descendants lost their writing), Sundaland (drowned, mythic shadows), and the Gulf (drowned, **with a surviving flood-literature corpus**). The Epic of Gilgamesh's Utnapishtim story — Ea's warning whispered through the reed wall, the equal-sided boat, the six-day flood, the mountain grounding, the dove, swallow, and raven — and Genesis after it are the only ice-age flood memories preserved in writing by the displaced themselves. Rose's closing observation deserves quotation: nearly every civilization of southern Mesopotamia told some form of the flood myth, from 2,500 BC through Genesis to the Qur'an, "consistent in content and structure." The drowned-world hypothesis with the best literary afterlife is also the one we could check by drilling — and haven't.

## 6.6 The rift lake: Lisan's collapse and shore-chasing peoples

The Jordan Rift held a giant lake through the glacial — **Lake Lisan**, the Pleistocene predecessor of the Dead Sea, filling the rift from Tiberias to the Khunaizer marshes. At its highstand around 26–24 ka it covered some 2,000 square kilometers — 220 kilometers long, 17–20 wide, standing ~270 meters above today's Dead Sea surface, hypersaline in the south but fresh enough in the north for hippos and aurochs. Its varved sediments, a summer layer of aragonite over a winter layer of detritus, give the Levant an annual-resolution climate clock; u-series-dateable shoreline aragonites track every rise and fall; its sediments record earthquakes as seismites and deep-water mass-transport deposits. The collapse between 14,000 and 13,000 BC — from highstand to roughly −500 meters within a thousand years — was the largest lake-level change in seventy millennia (tectonic assist debated), leaving first the shrunken Lake Damya (16.5–12 ka), then the Ghor: the flattened valley floor on which Jericho and the whole early Neolithic of the rift would later rise.

The archaeology maps the refugee shoreline: no Kebaran sites from the empty interval 17,000–13,500 BC below −203 meters (the lake swallowed them, or the people retreated); early Natufian camps cluster at −215 to −230 meters — shore-chasing camps on the receding margin, exactly the elevation logic of a lake falling fast. And on Lisan's last high shore, the Ohalo II camp (Part Nine) was preserved precisely because the lake rose over it: a village drowned in place at the freeze's end, lifted out of the mud by a modern drought. Three drowned lowlands — Sunda, the rift, the North Sea — and one pulse, MWP-1A, behind all their schedules.

## 6.7 The Aegean: two pulses, two standstills

Marine records from the northern Aegean register two abrupt freshenings and sea-level jumps — at 8.4 ka (the Agassiz outburst) and at **7.6 ka** (the Laurentide's final collapse) — with rapid rises of 1.4 and up to 4.5 meters on vulnerable coasts, pinned independently in the southwest Swedish and Rhine–Meuse Delta records, where meters of accommodation space were created in decades: enough to shift river mouths, drown marsh pasture, and redraw the map a community had memorized [S — Herrle et al., *Geology* 2018; PMC5840179]. The archaeology answers the pulses: two visible standstills in the spread of farming across southeast Europe, one after each event, as if the Neolithic frontier itself had to wait for the water to settle.

The pattern generalizes: coasts were simultaneously the hazard zone and the refuge. The event that drowned a farm concentrated its people on the remaining shore — the dynamic behind El Mazo's demographic growth (below) and behind every "refugee coast" of the early Holocene, from Franchthi's drowned Paralia to the Aegean island chain that the rising sea turned from a walkable shelf into the archipelago of the Odyssey's world.

## 6.8 Storegga and the 8.2 ka event: human verdicts, region by region

The 8.2 ka event is the Holocene's sharpest cold snap — the Agassiz-Ojibway outburst weakening Atlantic circulation, ~3.3°C of Northern Hemisphere cooling within about twenty years — and the Storegga tsunami arrived in the same centuries. Region by region, the human record shows a mosaic rather than a catastrophe:

- **Northern Spain (El Mazo cave)**: a Bayesian reconstruction from sixty-five radiocarbon dates and topshell isotopes shows sea-surface temperature crashing at 8,255–8,020 cal BP — and the *human* response was intensified marine foraging and demographic **growth** on the Atlantic coast, as the Ebro valley emptied toward the shore; warm-water topshells vanish from the menu, cold limpets dominate, harvests stay sustainable (large individuals, minimum sizes respected) throughout — "local marine ecological knowledge" operating under climate stress [S — García-Escárzaga et al. 2022].
- **Western Scotland**: Bayesian "activity events" show a dramatic population reduction [S — Wicks & Mithen 2014].
- **Fennoscandia**: no single collapse signature — but the whole forager *network* felt the event through its connections; a metapopulation-response model (27 studies surveyed) proposes the stress propagated through alliance structures even where the climate hit was mild [S — 2025 synthesis].
- **Denmark**: summed-probability growth stalls for three centuries (8.3–8.0 ka, on 485 dates), site counts dip, settlement shifts west-to-east, within a compound Storegga-plus-8.2ka stress decades before the Maglemosian–Kongemose transition — resilience read as repertoire change [S-adjacent].
- **The general warning**: a major SPD study of northwest Atlantic Europe finds no catastrophic regional decline around the event and explicitly warns against "sucking in and smearing" (Baillie's phrase) imprecisely dated global events onto local cultural change [S — Griffiths & Robinson 2017/2018].

The ice age's last lesson repeats: the same event, different refuges, opposite fates — determined by which side of the coastline your ancestors had bet on, and how strong their networks were when the bet was called.

## 7.1 Peopling the Americas: the standstill exits, the two routes, and the forensic roster

The founding of the Americas is the ice age's greatest migration, and its most contested. The genomic scaffold [S — Moreno-Mayar, Potter, Vinner et al., *Nature* 553:203, 2018; Raghavan et al. 2015; and successors]:

- The founding population split from East Asians 36±1.5 thousand years ago, exchanged genes until ~25±1.1 ka, took on roughly 40% Ancient North Eurasian ancestry 25–20 ka, and passed through its isolation bottleneck ~24,000 years ago — exactly when the LGM sealed Beringia's doors. Two scenarios remain open: the split in northeast Asia (preferred by the excavators, fitting an empty Siberian LGM record) or in Beringia itself (genetically parsimonious, given continuous gene flow thereafter). One methodological caution, voiced by Brennan Wygal among others: a dozen-odd early genomes sample a continent, and continent-scale humility is warranted. Two scenarios remain open:
- Around 20,900 years ago (22–18.1 ka range), the lineage divided: **Ancient Beringians** in the east, and the **Ancestral Native Americans** who would populate the continents. The split's location — Asia or the land bridge — is unresolved, and the answer decides whether the standstill even happened on American ground.
- The oldest securely dated Alaskan site, **Swan Point** (14,000–14,200 cal BP), preserves the moment of re-emergence: 11,500 artifacts — gray-green igneous rock, chert, rhyolite, chalcedony, basalt, and obsidian — including ~900 microblades and ten Yubetsu-method microcores (the Dyuktai toolkit of northeast Siberia carried across), three bifacial preforms, thirty-three burins, two hearths, and an ivory workshop processing low-nutrition ivory and swan wings: technical material, not food — proof that people and mammoths still overlapped, in a brief single-event occupation by highly mobile hunters [S — Holmes, Yesner et al.].
- At **Upward Sun River** (Tanana valley, interior Alaska, ~11,500 years old; excavated by Ben Potter's team in collaboration with the Healy Lake Village and the Tanana Chiefs Conference), a ceremonial burial pit held two infants — Xach'itee'aanenh T'eede Gaay ("Sunrise Child-girl," a six-to-twelve-week-old girl at 17-fold genome coverage) and Yełkaanenh T'eede Gaay ("Twilight Child-girl," prenatal) — buried together under ochre with salmon remains (the first evidence of salmon use in the New World) and a cremated three-year-old: the **Ancient Beringians**, defined. Genetically: mitochondrial C1b (USR1) and basal B2 (USR2 — the only other match is the ~9,000-year-old Trail Creek Cave individual); nuclear DNA shows the two were half-siblings to first cousins but maternally unrelated; no derived EDAR allele; a derived FADS2 (a selective sweep in progress). Ancient Beringians persisted in eastern Beringia to at least ~6,000 years ago (Trail Creek: the only known Denali-tradition human remains, slotted points and microblades matching USR), then were absorbed into incoming Dene speakers — the Northern Archaic — whose languages and genes (the Na-Dene linkage, Part Seven's Siberian ladder) still carry their trace [S — Moreno-Mayar et al. 2018; Potter et al. 2024; "Alaska was more part of Siberia than the Lower 48," as Charles Holmes put it: the Canadian ice shield blocked southward movement until ~13 ka].
- The ~12,500-year-old **Anzick child**, buried with Clovis artifacts in Montana, anchors the culture in the founding gene pool — his genome ties Clovis to the southern branch [S — Rasmussen et al. 2014]. Southward, the Ancestral Native Americans split into northern and southern branches ~17.5–14.6 ka (15.7 ka by the Karitiana/Athabascan SMC++ estimate), with Ancient-Beringian–southern and AB–northern gene flow persisting to ~10 and ~5 ka respectively — the genetic shadow of a corridor still open between cousins. The dispersal was so fast — both continents within two to three thousand years — that it implies pre-existing social infrastructure, not a blind walk into empty land [S-adjacent]. The kelp-highway coast and the newly opened interior corridor delivered people to Tierra del Fuego within that window; the interior corridor's own faunal poverty argues the coast carried the first wave.

**The forensic roster of early sites** — because this field runs on contested dates, here is the state of play as of 2026, graded:

- **Tier 1, strong**: Cooper's Ferry/Nipéhe, Idaho (Loren Davis's team; 16,560–15,280 cal BP on charcoal from an intact occupation; Western Stemmed points; the 2025 Crow Canyon synthesis extends the record to 16–13 ka; the Columbia River is the natural first off-ramp of a coastal migration — though Stuart Fiedel's savage rejoinder in *Lithic Technology* argues the oldest *demonstrated* occupation is ~11.5 ka and the luminescence "occupations" may be natural gravel bars; Davis answers with 2023–25 hunting-evidence papers). Paisley Caves, Oregon (Dennis Jenkins; ~14.3 ka; Shillito et al. 2020 independently confirmed three of six pre-Clovis "human" coprolites by fecal biomarkers — sterols and bile acids — on 285 radiocarbon dates and sixty-five coprolites; basketry directly dated to 12,273±56 BP; unresolved residue: the Washington State lab failed DNA extraction on eight coprolites where Copenhagen succeeded, three ancient coprolites carry an odd A2d1 Mexican clade, Goldberg questioned morphology, Poinar the leaching — the site's own lead says the evidence is strongest where independent lines converge). The Gault–Friedkin complex, Texas (Michael Waters: Gault Assemblage 16.7–21.7 ka by OSL on four samples, with no sterile layer between it and Clovis at 13.2–11.9 ka; "hard to explain away as artifact migration," in John Hawks's phrase — but Morrow's vertisol-mixing critique and Fiedel's Aubrey-analogue argument keep it defended; Friedkin's Buttermilk Creek Complex points at 13.5–15.5 ka, Gault Area 15 ~16 ka; Dillehay doubted Friedkin's integrity).
- **Tier 2, contended**: White Sands footprints (23–21 ka; Pigati et al. 2023: independent pollen radiocarbon and OSL, cool-wet pollen assemblage, blank corrections of ~2,200 14C years; 2025 mud-core dates of 20.7–22.4 ka; critics Rachal & Dello-Russo on formation processes and Ruppia ecology; the NPS-USGS-Bournemouth team's "at least 23,000 years" stands, contested). Bluefish Caves (24 ka; Part Five's full docket; Cave IV dates pending). Monte Verde, Chile — the keystone (Part Ten). Chiquihuite Cave, Zacatecas, Mexico (Ciprian Ardelean et al., *Nature* 2020: 1,930 limestone tools in layers of 25–32 ka, palm phytoliths, *no human DNA* — "a complete enigma... many failed colonizations," in Willerslev's phrase; James Chatters's critique — wrong cave area, insufficient lithic detail, behaviorally incompatible — and a reported 2021 geofact re-evaluation keep it C; Ardelean's defense: 239 tools sealed beneath the mud, learner-and-expert knapping marks).
- **Tier 3, rejected or media-only**: Pedra Furada (Franco-Brazilian reanalysis: natural percussion), Pikimachay, Tule Springs, Pendejo, Shriver (Fiedel's list); the Delmarva/Parsons Island claims of the Solutrean hypothesis (Part Ten).

One structural observation matters for the reader: every accepted pre-Clovis anchor has a dedicated professional skeptic (Fiedel across Paisley, Cooper's Ferry and the Delmarva claims; Chatters at Chiquihuite; Surovell at Monte Verde and the impact hypothesis), and every claim's survival depends on replication by the *other* camp. That adversarial refereeing, exhausting as it is, is why the roster above is worth more than any single headline — and the investigator-pattern (Surovell the serial establishment-challenger; Dillehay's fifty-year permit monopoly now under direct attack) is a subject in its own right, Part Thirteen.

## 7.2 Recolonizing Europe: the Magdalenian spring and the WHG expansion

As the ice pulled back, the children of the Franco-Cantabrian refuge walked out. The **Magdalenian** technocomplex (~17–12 ka) spread the GoyetQ2 genetic signature northeast across France, the Rhineland, and Britain; at **Gough's Cave** in Somerset (14,970–14,640 cal BP), the recolonizers of Britain — Magdalenians whose ancestors came from Belgium, per the GoyetQ2-affiliated genome of a woman from the cave — left the most intensively studied mortuary assemblage of the period: 205 human specimens (41 cranial), a minimum of six individuals, three skull-cups (one from a child of about three — the youngest known), facial and basal removal by controlled percussion, edges retouched, 65% of the bones bearing butchery marks and 42% human tooth-marks, marrow processing identical to horse and deer, and a radius engraved with a zigzag *after* the flesh was gone: ritual endocannibalism as a mortuary tradition, not starvation — the animal meat was abundant [S — Bello et al., *Journal of Human Evolution*; the wider complex includes Le Placard and Isturitz skull-cups, and at least a dozen cannibalism sites from Courbet to Hohle Fels]. Creswell Crags in Derbyshire records the whole arc: modern humans present more than 36,000 years ago (the Lincombian-Ranisian-Jerzmanowician blade industry — Ranis' British twin), Britain then abandoned for ten millennia as the LGM closed in, recolonized 16–15 ka (~35 Final Magdalenian sites, mostly south of Yorkshire to southern Scotland), and its first recognized cave art — the Church Hole engravings of ~13–12 ka [S — Bahn, Pettitt & Ripoll 2003; LRJ links to Ranis confirmed by Pettitt's raw-material work].

Then, around 14,000 years ago, the **Villabruna/Oberkassel** population — the western hunter-gatherers, modeled as ~75% Arene Candide (Liguria, 13 ka) + 25% GoyetQ2 (or 90/10 with Fournol) — expanded across western and central Europe in the Bølling-Allerød warmth: El Mirón (~19 ka, 43% Villabruna) the first pulse, Tagliente (~17 ka) the Balkan stream arriving early, Villabruna himself (14 ka, Y R1b1, mt U5b) the consolidation, Oberkassel (~13 ka, the famous double burial with its two dogs — among the oldest known deliberate canine burials) the expansion into Germany, Poland, Britain [S — Posth et al., *Nature* 2023; Fu et al. 2016 had caught the Near-East affinity appearing with the Bølling-Allerød ~14 ka; the 2026 Balkan preprint adds the deepest layer: the Villabruna world itself was substantially a Balkan export].

**Cheddar Man** (found 1903 in Gough's Cave, during cave drainage works; 8,540–7,990 BC; sequenced by the Natural History Museum–UCL–Trinity team, Ian Barnes and Selina Brace leading) is the file's portrait: ~85% Villabruna + 15% GoyetQ2 — dark-to-black skin by all then-known pigmentation loci, blue or green eyes, dark hair, lactose-intolerant, Y-chromosome I2a-I-S2524* (an extant line), mitochondrial U5b1 (the 2018 nuclear call superseding Bryan Sykes's 1996 PCR-based U5a — an early contamination lesson in one sentence), dead in his twenties with a possible violent lesion, buried alone in a cave where communal burial was the norm (Aveline's Hole held ~50 individuals in the same centuries). All British Mesolithic individuals are WHG, closest to the La Braña and Loschbour genomes; their persistent affinities to western European hunter-gatherers spanned Britain's separation from the continent. And the ending: farmers arrived ~4,000 BC (a thousand years late versus the continent) and replaced ~90% of the hunter-gatherer population, taking only ~10% admixture, geographically structured (Wales lowest) — with **no hunter-gatherer resurgence during the British Neolithic**, unlike the continent; roughly 10% of modern British ancestry traces to the WHG population, mostly via continental routes [S — Brace et al., *Nature Ecology & Evolution* 2019]. Adrian Targett, the Cheddar-area teacher whose mitochondrial DNA matched Cheddar Man's lineage in 1997, remains the popular emblem of that continuity — a matrilineal anecdote, not a population fact, and the document keeps the difference clear.

## 7.3 Recolonizing the cold: Siberia's ladder

Siberia after the LGM refilled from the south, time-transgressively [S — Graf & Buvit's synthesis, the current standard]: the Enisei and Transbaikal were abandoned or invisible 24.8–22.7 ka, then the Transbaikal hearths of Studenoe 2 and Ust'-Menza 2 reignited at 22.7–20.9 ka (hearths laid in alluvium), the upper Enisei (Nizhnii Idzhir') followed, the Aldan valley by 17–15.5 ka, Alaska (Swan Point) by 14 ka. Microblades — the LGM's great technological answer, standardized, planned, insurance against failure — had their conceptual roots in Hokkaido 27–25 ka and moved northwest and northeast with the warming.

East of the Lena, the post-glacial sequence becomes a named ladder with genomes on every rung [S — Zeng et al. 2025, PMC12342343, and the archaeological literature]: **Sumnagin** (Mesolithic, 13–7 ka; conical cores replace wedge-shaped; retouched microblades) → **Syalakh** (early Neolithic, 7,000–5,700 BC; net-imprinted pottery, polished tools, bone harpoons, the bow, moose iconography, and the appearance of mode-5 lever pressure-flaking) → **Belkachi** (5,700–4,300 BC; cord-and-zigzag pottery, grooved points with hafted microblade insets) → **Ymyyakhtakh** (4,300–3,300 BC; waffle-imprint ceramics, bronze arriving). The genomic mapping: Khatystyr Cave's Middle Lena individual (10.2 ka) is the earliest strongly East-Asian genome in Siberia (Amur-related); Kolyma_M at 10.1 ka; Dzhilinda 8.4 ka; the Syalakh-Belkachi people (6.8–6.2 ka) shift toward Bering Strait populations while taking ~20% Baikal East-Asian admixture and later ~50% Transbaikal Kitoi admixture into the Yakutia_LNBA/Ymyyakhtakh mix — and, the headline, contribute roughly **70% of Paleo-Eskimo (Ancient Beringian–Saqqaq–Dorset) ancestry**, persisting in every Bering Strait population (Eskimo-Aleut, Chukotko-Kamchatkan, Yukaghir). Flegontov et al. 2016 adds the second migration: Na-Dene speakers descend from a later Paleo-Eskimo movement, and the **Dené-Yeniseian hypothesis** — Edward Vajda's demonstration of a Ket–Na-Dene relationship at roughly 4–6 ka, the deepest claimed language relationship on Earth — attaches the Ket language of the Yenisei to this same eastern-Siberian complex; the Uralic spread, separately, tracks the Yakutia_LNBA dispersal ~4 ka (the Kra001 outlier individual). Siberia's refuge peoples became the Americas' second founding.

## 7.4 Fennoscandia and the Mediterranean: two frontiers, one lesson

Scandinavia's pioneers (Part Five) met a land still rising from its ice, rebounding tens of meters per millennium — a coast where the oldest settlements now lie inland and the shoreline of the first landfalls is high and dry. In Britain the pioneer signature is Star Carr's red-deer antler frontlets (9,300–8,500 BC; 33 known, two or more per major site, engineered with drilled holes and smoothed skull-vaults — group-identity badges for communities re-founding a deglaciated world, in the Wild-Gehlen-Street synthesis, against Clark's shaman and Pratsch's hunting-disguise readings; the site also preserves Britain's earliest bow, its earliest carpentry in a wooden platform, and its oldest Mesolithic art in an engraved shale pendant) [S — Clark; Milner, Conneller & Taylor; Mellars's polemic "Moonshine over Star Carr" marks the debate's temperature] — and Howick's 7,800-BC house (Waddington's excavations) with its birch frame and turf roof, among the oldest in the islands, its foreshore footprints preserved in the intertidal peat.

The Mediterranean's mariners met a sea falling away from their caves. At **Franchthi** (the Argolid; excavated by Thomas Jacobsen from 1967), people who had sporadically visited through the Upper Palaeolithic returned after a 4,000-year LGM-Younger Dryas hiatus as a fully maritime culture: bladelet hunters of wild ass and red deer became tuna-fishers (the deep-sea claim debated against shallow-herding skepticism) and obsidian seafarers, crossing 90 nautical miles of open water to Melos by ~11,000 BC — "the earliest clear documentation of seafaring in the world" (Jacobsen; Curtis Runnels's Mesolithic reads it as an intrusive coastal culture unconnected with the Upper Palaeolithic layers); their shoreline settlement, Paralia, now lies under the Argolic Gulf, drowned by the very rise that made their voyages necessary; agriculture arrived by boat ~7,000 BC, PPNB emigrants carrying emmer, lentil, and ovicaprids, and the first farmers burned their dead among shell middens. From Cyprus to Sardinia to the Adriatic's Vlakno cave (and the seafaring ladder of Part Five), the early Holocene is a litany of island first-landfalls.

The lesson both frontiers teach: *the end of the ice age did not end the refugee condition — it merely changed what you needed to flee across.* Ice first. Then water. Then, as always, each other.

## 8.1 The machine that reads the dead

Ancient DNA is a technology story before it is a science story, and its founding text is a retraction. In 1984 Svante Pääbo announced DNA from an Egyptian mummy; the result was later acknowledged as modern contamination, and the field he went on to build carries that humility in its protocols [S — Pääbo's own accounts]. With Allan Wilson at Berkeley he performed the first PCR on ancient remains in 1988; at LMU Munich in the 1990s he attacked the hardest target available — the Neanderthals.

The method's standards were set by the first Neanderthal mitochondrial sequence in 1997 (Krings et al., *Cell*): 379 nucleotides from the Feldhofer specimen, with material sent blind to Mark Stoneking's Penn State lab for independent replication — the two-lab protocol that still defines credible work, and the result that placed Neanderthal mitochondria outside modern human variation (a split estimated then at 550–690 ka, a mitochondrial common ancestor at 120–150 ka). The Max Planck Institute for Evolutionary Anthropology, which Pääbo helped found in Leipzig in 1997, then produced the sequence of discoveries this document rests on: the first million nucleotides of Neanderthal nuclear DNA in 2006 (454 pyrosequencing, with a parallel metagenomic effort later shown to carry over 11 percent human contamination — the finding that spurred clean-room extraction and Neanderthal-specific adapters); the 2010 draft Neanderthal genome (Green et al., *Science*), which proved Neanderthals equally close to Europeans and East Asians and closer to non-Africans — the admixture discovery; the 2010 mitochondrial genome from a finger bone at Denisova Cave that revealed an entirely unknown species (Krause et al.); Matthias Meyer's single-stranded library that delivered the 30x Denisovan genome in 2012; the 2016 finding that the 430,000-year-old people of Sima de los Huesos were early Neanderthals carrying mysteriously Denisovan-like mitochondria; the 2018 discovery of "Denny," the first-generation hybrid with a Neanderthal mother and a Denisovan father (Slon et al.); and, from 2019, the **sediment revolution** — human DNA recovered from cave dirt itself, no bones required, the technique that has since read the Ebro's Solutrean centuries (Part Five), Denisova's occupation layers, and Doggerland's drowned rivers (Part Six) [S — composite of the primary literature]. The 2017 high-coverage Vindija genome (1.6 heterozygous sites per 10,000 — lower than any modern population) quantified how thin their blood had run; the 2018 hypochlorite-decontamination method (Kelso and colleagues) unlocked five late Neanderthals (39–47 ka) and exposed a *late turnover* — eastern early lineages replaced by western-related ones (Part Eleven). Pääbo received the 2022 Nobel Prize in Physiology or Medicine "for his discoveries concerning the genomes of extinct hominins and human evolution"; per Ewen Callaway's *Nature* profile, he "helped spawn the competitive field" — the factions this document keeps naming: Leipzig (Krause, now at Jena; Prüfer), Harvard (Reich), Copenhagen (Willerslev), Uppsala–Tübingen–Stockholm (Jakobsson, Posth, Dalén), and the China–Frankfurt axis (Fu, Yu).

Two cautionary tales belong here for any reader who trusts museum objects. The Zlatý kůň skull was contaminated by the cattle-bone glue a conservator had used generations before the sequencers arrived, its bovine mitochondrial DNA announcing the forgery of its own preparation [S — Prüfer et al. 2021]. The Königsaue birch tar dated thousands of years too old until someone identified phthalate plasticizer from modern handling — a contamination overestimate of more than four thousand years [S — Koller et al. 2001]. The dead are honest; their keepers, sometimes, are not. If Part Eight is your first contact with genetics, read it slowly: every technical term is defined where it first appears, and Appendix B is the safety net — the prose here does the same work the pedigree tags do throughout, separating what we know from how we know it.

## 8.2 The great chain of replacements (and survivals)

Compress the European record into one paragraph and it reads as a relay of refuges: the first arrivals (Bacho Kiro, Zlatý kůň, Ust'-Ishim, Ranis) largely failed and left no descendants; the Gravettian ran on two peoples (Fournol-west, Věstonice-east, Part Three); the LGM kept the southwest — the Fournol→Solutrean→Magdalenian GoyetQ2 line — while Italy's Gravettians died out entirely; the Villabruna/Oberkassel population — itself substantially Balkan in origin (Part Five) — then expanded across the west in the Bølling-Allerød warmth; farmers replaced ~90% of them in most places ~8,000 years ago, taking ~10% admixture, structured by geography (Wales lowest) and with *no* hunter-gatherer resurgence in Britain; steppe pastoralists replaced much of *that* ~4,500 years ago (Yamnaya ~48:52 farmer:steppe in the 1,000-ancient-European-genomes models; later Europeans ~15:85 Bronze-Age:earlier) [S — the 1,000-genomes selection study, *Nature* 2022, PMC9435429; the epoch models; IRX3 obesity selection at the Neolithic transition included]. Yet survivals thread the chain: Iberia's paternal lineage C-V20 persists in modern Spain; ~15% of Cheddar Man's genome was Magdalenian residue; and in the Balkan preprint's long reach, the refuge that seeded the steppe got the last word through Yamnaya's descendants, who carried ~16% Epi-Balkan ancestry back across Europe and into Central and South Asia. Replacement is the rule; nothing is ever total; and every survivor is a braided remnant of everyone who failed.

## 8.3 The ANE triangle: Siberia's ice-age heirs

The Ancient North Eurasian complex is the ice age's most consequential lineage, and its male genealogy is a forensic chain: the Yana men carry Y-haplogroup **P-M45** (P-FT344715 and P-FT344716); the Mal'ta boy carries basal **R**; Afontova Gora-2 carries **Q** [S — the published phylogenies]. R and Q are both sons of P — born, the YFull reasoning goes, among ANE in Siberia — and R2 passed through a ~12,000-year bottleneck with 189 equivalent SNPs against R-M269's 97: one surviving paternal line through the freeze. The autosomal story: ANE formed by the merger of an Early West Eurasian population (Kostenki-14-like, the "northern route") and an Early East Eurasian one (Tianyuan-like, the "southern route"), estimated 68–73 percent and 27–32 percent respectively depending on the model, with the Salkhit skull (34 ka, Mongolia) showing the related complex eastward; the chain Yana → Mal'ta → Afontova Gora runs through the LGM — "the LGM cleared the slate; one subset of ANE repopulated Siberia" — and the Afontova Gora-3 individual (18 ka, Krasnoyarsk) is the earliest known carrier of the blond-hair allele KITLG (Mathieson et al. 2018 supplementary), later common in Samara, Motala, and Ukrainian hunter-gatherers but absent in western ones: blond, on current evidence, is an ANE inheritance [S — with the forum-grade steps flagged where used].

Its fractions in later peoples are the headline: roughly 30–40 percent of first-wave Native Americans (up to ~41 percent in early Andean genomes), about half of Yamnaya, 21 percent in the Ainu, 10–25 percent in modern Europeans via the eastern hunter-gatherers (25–50 percent in the EHG→Yamnaya conduit, per Flegontov et al. 2016), ~40 percent in the Kets — the modern maximum — and a staggering **72 percent in the Bronze Age Tarim mummies** [S — Zhang et al., *Nature* 599:256, 2021, PMC8580821]. The Tarin file deserves its sentences: thirteen individuals from the Xiaohe cemetery (2,135–1,623 BCE; the rediscovered 1934 Bergman cemetery, found again in 2000), qpAdm-modeled as 72 percent Afontova-Gora-3-like ANE plus 28 percent Baikal early-Bronze-age ancestry, with **zero** Western Steppe Herder input — a genetically isolated population that adopted neighboring agropastoralism while sitting on the ANE relict's homeland, "the best representatives" of ANE despite fourteen thousand years of distance: the refugee lineage that stayed home. Their Y chromosomes (R1b-FTC300) descend from the ANE R line; the 2025 follow-up adds the LBA far-west Tarim (1,600–1,400 BCE) as Sintashta-Andronovo plus 10 percent BMAC plus 12 percent of the earlier Tarim layer — the second wave arriving over the first, as always. The horse that wasn't (Haringtonhippus) and the dog that was (below) complete the ANE-era bestiary; and the Deer Tooth pendant of Denisova Cave (~24.7 ka, an ANE woman) is the lineage's smallest artifact.

## 8.4 The admixture clock: meeting the locals

When did sapiens and Neanderthals actually meet, and how? The field's best current answer [S — Iasi, Chintalapati, Skov, Hajdinjak, Peter & Moorjani, *Science* 2024, adq3010; 59 ancient and 275 modern genomes]: a **single extended pulse between 50,500 and 43,500 years ago**, mean ~47,000 — with selection purging Neanderthal ancestry from introgressed segments within about a hundred generations of each admixture, so the "Neanderthal deserts" (long genomic regions empty of archaic ancestry) formed almost immediately, consistent with many Neanderthal variants being mildly harmful on a sapiens background. The higher Neanderthal fraction in East Asians reflects dilution history, not a second pulse. The implication set is sharp: the out-of-Africa expansion was essentially complete by 43.5 ka; sapiens and Neanderthals coexisted side by side for some six to seven thousand years (matching the archaeological window); and the receiving population was structured, which the authors flag.

An independent 2026 study of the X chromosome added a socially loaded finding [S — Platt, Harris & Tishkoff, *Science* 391(6788):922–925, 2026-02-26]. Neanderthal X chromosomes carry a **62 percent relative excess of sapiens ancestry** — the mirror image of the human-side desert — and the surplus segments are poor in functional elements, which rules out selection (the toxicity theory would purge in both directions). The explanation ranking: mate preference, predominantly **male Neanderthals and female sapiens, persisting across both interbreeding episodes** ~200,000 years apart — even an all-female migrant pool explains only 1.3-fold of the observed 1.6. The researchers declined to speculate on consent; commentary ranged from "male-monopolization patterns are hard to square with friendly interaction" (Churchill) to "females are typically the choosier sex, and there is no violence signature in the archaeology" (Nowell). The claim, and both cautions, belong in any honest account — as does the methodological irony that the deserts, once read as biological incompatibility, now read as a mating pattern.

Denisovan admixture runs later and messier: 44–54 ka in the first estimates (Sankararaman 2016), with Oceania receiving 4–6 percent through *multiple distinct pulses* (Part Eleven), South Asia elevated under dilution-versus-multi-pulse debate, and the Sherpa carrying the highest fraction outside Oceania. And the direction of flow was sometimes reversed: Li, Akey and colleagues (*Science* 385:eadi1768, 2024) found **modern-human DNA introgressing into Neanderthals** — 5–10 percent at 200–250 ka into the Altai lineage (the "ghost modern" in Neanderthals, echoing the Misliya-era presence) plus a later ~0.5 percent pulse into the Vindija/Chagyrskaya lineages. Masking those human-introgressed stretches *shrinks* Neanderthal effective-population estimates by a quarter to a third (Altai 3,369→2,484; Chagyrskaya 2,964→2,379; Vindija 3,408→2,807): the Neanderthals, too, were partly us, and their famously thin genetic diversity was partly a misreading of our own genes in their bones.

## 8.5 Ghosts and deep structure: the never-found

The ghost question is treated in full in Part Two; its West Eurasian chapter belongs here. **"Basal Eurasian"** — a lineage minimally admixed with Neanderthals, demanded by every West-Eurasian admixture model since 2014 — survives as a formal necessity and a physical absence: no unadmixed specimen has ever been published. The 2025–26 consensus reading (flagged B-grade, as it travels partly through community synthesis) equates it with Lazaridis's 2018 "ANA" — a North-African-resident Eurasian-branch lineage — places its admixture pre-out-of-Africa, and notes that the Ranis–Zlatý kůň people are *not* it (a deeper, pre-split relic, per Sümer 2025); Vallini's 2024 model assigns it ~18 percent of CHG. Every qpAdm tree still demands a pre-OOA-divergence source; F6 statistics insist. Until someone sequences it, "Basal Eurasian" is the most useful ghost in the field — and the Dzudzuana preprint (Part Five) remains its most-cited candidate, eight years unpublished.

The older ghosts are consolidating rather than multiplying: Durvasula's West African signal folds into TRACE's universal ~830 ka layer (Part Two); the super-archaic *erectus*-grade ancestry in Oceanians arrives via Denisovan intermediaries, not directly; and Roger et al.'s two-event super-archaic history (≥2 Ma separation; the ~350 ka erectus-into-Denisovan pulse) frames the deepest layer anyone has dated. **The write-up rule for this whole territory:** "ghost" claims always travel with their confidence intervals, their methods, and their authors' clarifications. In this field the ghosts are as real as the fossils — and as demanding of humility.

## 8.6 Sahul's clock and the deep-ancestry wall

The peopling of Australia and New Guinea is anchored by one of the field's most elegant papers [S — Malaspinas et al., *Nature* 538:207, 2016; 83 Aboriginal Australian and 25 Papuan highland genomes, high coverage; the Willerslev lab]. Its findings, in order of confidence: a single founding population (the sampled Australians all descend from one founding population that differentiated internally 10–32 ka); divergence from Eurasians 51–72 ka under a single-out-of-Africa-with-archaic-admixture model; Denisovan admixture ~4.0 percent (CI 3.3–5.0) at ~44 ka (CI 31–50) — *before* the Australian–Papuan split; a primary Neanderthal pulse of 2.3 percent ~60 ka (CI 55–84) plus a secondary 1.1 percent into Eurasians only; the Australian–Papuan diversification at 25–40 ka — plausibly driven by the LGM's hyper-arid interior and the rise of the landlocked **Carpentarian lake** ~40 ka when the sea fell below the 53-meter Arafura Sill, a lake that later salinized and isolated its shorelines; and the Holocene northeastward expansion ~10 ka as the Pama-Nyungan phenomenon — cultural replacement with limited gene flow, "a few immigrants who changed the way people spoke and thought — just to disappear again like ghosts." The 2025 refinement [S — Mondal et al., via Hawks's September 2025 review]: the Papuan-mainland split at 46.2 ka, a distinct Denisovan pulse into Papuans at ~31.3 ka, and the death of the 2016 Pagani "xOoA" early-exit ghost.

Two extremes frame the region's isolation spectrum. Deep mitochondrial coalescences in highland New Guinea (80–122 ka; Redd 1999 and successors) testify to the region's demographic depth, with the standing caveat that archaic-pulse inflation inflates such estimates. And the Andaman arc holds the complementary extreme: a people reduced by colonial-era collapse from thirteen linguistic groups in the mid-nineteenth century to three survivors — Jarawa, Onge, and the Sentinelese, the last protected to this day by their own hostility and never sampled, an ethical bright line — whose Victorian-era skeletal collections became the genomic reference for deep human isolation [S — Thangaraj et al. 2003 and successors].

The deep-ancestry wall stands unreconciled: genomics insists no successful out-of-Africa population existed before ~50 ka (Sümer 2025's corollary), while archaeology keeps producing sites older than that — Madjedbebe's contested 65 ka, Tam Pà Ling's deep layers, Sulawesi's art (Part Nine), the Fuyan/Zhiren claims. Hawks's pointed speculation — *Denisovans may have been the ones there first* — is flagged as speculation. This is the live tension at the center of Eurasian prehistory as of September 2026, and it is stated here rather than resolved because honesty is the method. Australian Aboriginal genomes hold the other pole of the modern distribution — the highest structural-variant diversity ever measured in an isolated population (some 160,000 unique variants in one 2023 study of 121 individuals; Reis et al., *Nature* 623:1), founder effects explaining the world's highest Machado-Joseph disease prevalence (ATXN3), and selection signals catalogued in the 2023 deep-structure paper — the ice age's small-population arithmetic still legible, clinic by clinic.

## 8.7 Our fellow travelers: dogs and horses

The ice age domesticated, or nearly domesticated, its camp followers, and the dog's story is a mirror of our own with one extra twist [S/C composite]. The genomic frame: Bergström et al. 2020 (*Science* 370:557; 27 ancient dog genomes) — all dogs share a common origin in an *extinct* wolf population, with limited wolf→dog but substantial dog→wolf gene flow, at least five major lineages diversified by 11 ka, and a co-analysis with humans that mirrors our history (Levant-related ancestry in African and early-European-farmer dogs) while differing from it (near-absent steppe impact; complete turnover of Neolithic European dog ancestry). Bergström et al. 2022 (*Nature*; 72 ancient wolf genomes back to 100 ka) refined the origin: dogs derive ancestry from at least **two** wolf populations — an eastern source in all dogs, a western (Middle-East-related) source in Middle Eastern, African, and southern European dogs only — the choice between dual domestication and single-origin-plus-admixture unresolved; dogs are closest to eastern wolves, and the wolves' own ice-age survival ran on continent-wide connectivity, with selection sweeps visible across thirty thousand generations. Frantz et al. 2016 (*Science*; 59 ancient dogs, Newgrange at 28x) had made the dual-origin case earlier: a deep east-west dog split dated 14–6.4 ka, eastern dogs partially replacing Europe's Palaeolithic dogs, and a mitochondrial discontinuity (ancient Europe 63 percent haplogroup C and 20 percent D; modern Europe 64 percent A and 22 percent B) with Newgrange carrying a "ghost" component outside modern dog-and-wolf variation. Against this, the Palaeolithic "dogs" of Germonpré's series (Goyet ~35.5 ka cal, Aurignacian, its mtDNA sister to all dogs+wolves with no descendants; Předmostí 28.5 ka with durophagy microwear; Mezhirich 4493; Mezin 5490; Eliseevichi 447 and 23781 at 16.5 ka) may be an extinct western lineage or abortive domestications, morphologically contested to this day — the Goyet skull is "almost certainly not a dog, likely a female wolf" per its morphometric critics (Boudadi-Maligne & Escarguel 2014; Drake 2015; Janssens 2019), against the Brussels school's randomization test (Galeta et al. 2021, *Anatomical Record*) and cultural evidence (perforated braincases, special diets, burial proximity). Germonpré's social-domestication model — foragers esteemed carnivores, kept wolf pups, and let the docile breed — reads the skull perforations as ritual treatment, "honorable guests" in Rane Willerslev/Losey's framing. Perri et al. 2021 (*PNAS*) close the American file: dogs entered the Americas *with* the first peoples, and the Paisley coprolites' canid DNA records early interbreeding with native canids.

The horse that wasn't: the stilt-legged horses of Pleistocene America were a separate genus, **Haringtonhippus francisci** — sister to all living *Equus* (with Hippidion as sister to both), diverging 4.1–5.8 Ma, stilt-leggedness evolved *convergently* with Asiatic asses, a morphological lie the genomics corrected (Heintzman et al., *eLife* 2017, cited 99; the Klondike placer-mining community supplied the Yukon specimens) [S]. They survived *through* the LGM in eastern Beringia — three concordant dates ~14.4 ka overturning the old last-appearance of 31.4 ka — and on the continent to 13.1 ka (Gypsum Cave, Nevada), ranging Mexico to Alberta, sympatric with caballine horses both sides of the ice; Natural Trap Cave's ancient DNA shows horse and camel gene flow through the ice-free corridor, and the 2019 synonymization paper (nesting it back in *Equus*) plus 2026 cheek-tooth morphometrics keep the genus question technically alive. Both American horse genera — born on the continent, extinguished within about a millennium of first human contact — left the horse to return in 1493 as an invader of its own homeland.

## 8.8 Bottlenecks you can diagnose: the medical afterglow

Refugium logic still writes prescriptions, and the Finnish case is its textbook [S — Peltonen et al., *Human Molecular Genetics* 8:1913, 1999, cited 602; Norio's 1960s–2003 series; de la Chapelle's laboratory]. The **Finnish Disease Heritage** comprises three dozen recessive-dominated disorders — congenital nephrotic syndrome (CNF), cornea plana 2, Imerslund-Gräsbeck, Unverricht-Lundborg (EPM1), APECED, lysinuric protein intolerance, aspartylglucosaminuria (AGU), Usher 3, Meretoja amyloidosis, Mulibrey nanism, PLOSL/Hakola, cartilage-hair hypoplasia — fixed at 70–100 percent single-founder frequencies (the TWNK c.1523A>G mutation is 93 times more frequent in Finns than elsewhere, SLC7A7 65 times, CRADD 50 times), the signature of stacked bottlenecks ~4,000 and ~2,000 years ago and the 16th-century "late settlement" sub-isolates of the east. The detection history is the science in miniature: Sajantila et al. 1996 (*PNAS*) found the founding bottleneck in mitochondrial DNA; Kittles et al. 1999 (*AJPG*) found it male-specific — essentially **two surviving Y-chromosome founder lineages** (N1c from the east, I1 from the west, the dual origin again); Lahermo 1999 mapped the founding lineages. The modern sequel: FinnGen's half-million-participant biobank converted the founder-effect liability into one of pharmacogenetics' richest mapping resources; Lim et al. 2014 quantified the enriched loss-of-function load; and a 2026 revisionist reading (community-grade, flagged) notes that the Bolshoy Oleni Siberian component (~46.5 percent by 1,500 BCE, per Lamnidis) *predates* the disease bottleneck, so the modern gradient (5.9 percent Siberian in the southwest, 8.9 percent in the east) records two stacked events plus dilution, not one migration.

The Sámi carry the complementary signatures: mitochondrial V and U5b1b1 at 80 percent of their maternal lines — a *western*-European origin for most of them, the post-glacial recolonization remembered — the world's highest frequency of the meat-diet-adapted AGT Pro11Leu variant (27.9 percent, against 2.3 percent in Chinese; the glyoxylate-detoxification hypothesis), and APOE*4 at 31 percent (against 5.2 percent in long-agricultural Greeks) — a forager lipid profile exposed by Westernization; early studies found low cardiovascular disease and most cancers, the protection evaporating in modern data (with the 1986 Chernobyl dietary-shift confound honestly noted), asthma and allergy running twice the Norwegian baseline. And the Aboriginal Australian file holds the diversity pole: the highest structural-variant load ever measured in an isolated community (Part Eight), founder-effect disease at world maxima — and through it all, the oldest continuous cultural tradition on Earth. The ice age's arithmetic — small groups, isolation, drift — is still readable, clinic by clinic; founder populations are research assets and biomedical-colonialism risks at once, a tension the governance chapter (Part Twelve) takes up directly.

## 9.1 Art: two birthplaces and a dating war

**The two birthplaces.** Figurative art appears at both ends of the ice-age world within a few thousand years, and the Sunda Shelf thesis connects them: if coastal dispersal carried the capacity, most of the early art is now underwater, and the surviving karst fragments on opposite sides of the drowned continent are its remaining witnesses. In Europe, the Chauvet lions and horses (phase one, 37–33.5 ka) and El Castillo's red disk (~40.8 ka minimum) hold the early record. In Sulawesi, a warty pig at Leang Tedongnge is dated to at least **45,500 years ago** (a 136-by-54-centimeter adult male *Sus celebensis*, his crest and facial warts drawn in dark red ochre, positioned above two others as if watching them fight, with two hand stencils over his hindquarters; the minimum age comes from cave popcorn accreted over his rear foot), a therianthrope hunting scene at Leang Bulu' Sipong 4 (~43.9 ka), hand stencils at ~39.9 ka — and, as of 2026, a "claw-finger" stencil on Muna Island at a minimum of **67,800 years** (Oktaviana, Joannes-Boyau, Hakim, Aubert, Brumm; the narrowed, claw-like fingers are a Sulawesi-only style, matched at Leang Jarie and Pettakere; laser-ablation U-series at Southern Cross University; Brumm's comment: Madjedbebe's art "most likely inherited from their ancestors in Nusantara") [S with the caveats below].

**The dating war.** The uranium-thorium method that produced these dates — measuring radioactive decay in the thin calcite skins that grow over paintings — is now in open battle, and every reader should know the shape of the fight [C — both sides documented, round by round]. Round one (2015): Sauvet and colleagues in *Quaternary International* called the method experimental, with unsupportable uncertainties. Round two (2016–17): Pike, Aubert, and colleagues answered in the same journal that the critics' premises were wrong — speleothems did not stop growing in glacial times (MIS-3 growth documented, even MIS-2), detrital thorium can be measured rather than assumed, and for cave art U-Th "is currently the most reliable of available chronometric techniques"; the counter to radiocarbon, they noted, is that old charcoal on cave floors need not relate to the paintings above. Round three (2018): the *Science* papers dating Neanderthal-era art — La Pasiega's ladder at 64.8 ka, Ardales' flowstone-pierced stalagmite at 65.5 ka, Maltravieso's hand stencil at 66.7 ka — with Aubert's team contesting attribution (pigment of uncertain origin; stratigraphic demonstration incomplete) and the Pike team answering with chlorine-36 and cross-laboratory checks (their methods paper: Hoffmann et al., *Quaternary Geochronology* 36:104). Round four (2026): Sauvet's renewal in *Annals of History and Archaeological Anthropological Explorations*, armed with specific failures: at Nerja, one calcite yields three mutually impossible ages (U-Th ~119 ka; associated charcoal ~19 ka; the calcite's *own* carbon-14 ~14 ka); at Leang Balangajia in Sulawesi, the published table itself shows an underlying layer dated **82.13 ka** beneath overlying growth of 29–45 ka (LBLGJ2.5: 72.6 ka beneath a 32.3 ka overlying layer) — inversions that imply uranium loss; and at Leang Tedongnge the standard stratigraphic safeguard failed, three slices through the crust returning statistically identical ages (45.44, 46.40, 43.88 ka), though the authors had read that very uniformity as closed-system proof. The Pike camp's defense — strict lab protocols, stratigraphic corroboration, the 259-date Chauvet corpus — stands; the internal contradictions in the published tables are now part of the public record, and the coverage (LiveScience, May 2026; *Archaeology Magazine*; the ancient-art press) has made the dispute general knowledge. **The writing rule for this document: every cave-art date travels with its method and its caveat** — the same discipline the genetics sections apply to contested claims.

**The European corpus, war by war.** *Chauvet*: the most intensively dated decorated cave on Earth — 259 radiocarbon dates in Quiles et al. 2016 (*PNAS*), two occupations (phase one 37.0–33.5 ka, most of the black drawings; a 2.3–3.5 kyr gap; phase two 31.4–28.8 ka, torch marks, two drawings, a child's footprints), cave bears using the cave to 33 ka, the Abraham Pillar rockfall ending phase one at 34.5±2.0 ka, the E1 collapse ending phase two at 29.4±1.8, against which the stylistic objection (Pettitt, Bahn, Züchner, Jouve: the art "looks" Gravettian/Magdalenian and must be that young) has never found a chronometric foothold; IntCal20 recalibration (2020) pushes the oldest painting to ~36.5 ka. *Lascaux*: after a seventy-year war — Breuil and Peyrony's 1940s Gravettian guess; the 1951 Libby laboratory date of 15,516±900; Glory's 17,190±140 and 16,000±500; Leroi-Gourhan's stylistic Magdalenian II; the 1998–2002 AMS antler dates of 18,600±190 and 18,930±230 (= 23,500–22,000 cal BP, Upper Solutrean–Badegoulian); and the LAsCO project (2018–21), which redated the cave's use to 1,000–1,500 years later than the 1990s readings and fixed the official chronology at ~23 ka, Solutrean painters, by Norbert Aujoulat's formal analysis (the confronted ibex and the Shaft scene's bird match Roc-de-Sers and Fourneau-du-Diable bas-reliefs; the horses match Fourneau) — the "17,000-year-old Lascaux" of every textbook is retired. *Altamira*: the polychrome bison ceiling (~15,000–13,000 BC) was re-confirmed in 2026 by Pons-Branchu and colleagues' cross-dating — U-Th against direct AMS radiocarbon of the bison charcoal itself — resolving the standoff with Pike 2012's crust dates (red claviform 36.16±0.61 ka; dotted-outline horse 22.11±0.13 ka); the occupation stack runs Gravettian base ~20 ka, two Solutrean layers (17,600–15,200 BC), five Magdalenian (13,500–12,000 BC), a rockfall sealing the cave ~13 ka; the discovery story keeps its own lesson (Marcelino Sanz de Sautuola and his eight-year-old daughter María in 1879; the decade-long "forgery" scandal; exoneration in 1902; the mold-closure and the Neocave replica). *Cosquer*: the drowned gallery — entrance 37 meters down, a 175-meter dive tunnel, three divers dead in 1991, the finder Henri Cosquer spending years proving his discovery — holds two phases (33–29 ka: sixty-five hand stencils, mostly female, many with missing finger joints; 25–20 ka: animals, signs, hearths), 177 animals (63 horses, 28 ibex, 17 deer, 10 bison, 7 aurochs) of which sixteen are marine — nine monk seals, three great auks, a jellyfish — the "killed man" half-seal figure, and four-fifths of the cave now submerged; the artists painted it 6–10 km from a sea like Iceland's, on steppe with Scots pine, and the Clottes–Courtin–Collina-Girard documentation standing behind it all; a full replica (Cosquer Méditerranée) opened in 2022. *El Castillo*: Pike 2012's disk (corrected 41.40±0.57 ka) and stencil (37.3 ka), Zilhão's rule that >42 ka in Europe means Neanderthal authorship, Pat Shipman's counter — why would Neanderthals wait 300,000 years to start painting exactly when sapiens arrived? — and the 2016 technology study showing the disks were made by many different people, by different blow-and-smear techniques, with different pigment recipes: repeated visits by different communities, not one atelier [S — with the 2026 caveats applied]. (The site's deeper claims — Obermaier's 1910–14 sequence and the ~150,000-year 'Pilgrim Map' — remain unconfirmed.)

**The African file, honestly mapped.** Deeper time supplies the antecedents the comparison lacks: Blombos Cave (100,000–72,000 years ago) preserves cross-hatched engraved ochre, pierced shell beads with use-wear, a hundred-thousand-year-old ochre-processing workshop mixed in abalone-shell pots, and Still Bay points heat-treated and pressure-flaked seventy-five millennia before the LGM [S — Henshilwood, d'Errico and colleagues] — the deep African toolkit the refugia carried into the freeze. What survives from the LGM itself is thin, vandalized, and mostly lost: Apollo 11 Cave (Wendt, 1969–72) holds seven buried quartzite slabs with charcoal-and-ochre paintings (~27.5–27.7 ka; the famous slab is a therianthrope — human hind legs, antelope body and horns, feline head), occupational pulses running 71–29 ka, and was vandalized in 2007, unprotected; Qurta's bovid petroglyphs (OSL 10±1 to 16±2 ka) are the only secure Pleistocene figurative rock art in North Africa; the Cave of Bees preserves sixteen painted spalls in 12.5–15 ka sediments — the minimum ages of lost wall art; Wonderwerk's engraved plaque (>276 ka, U-series, seven deliberate lines) pushes the engraving tradition itself into deep time. Robert Bednarik's taphonomic law explains the poverty: minerals outlast eggshell outlast bone outlast paint outlast wood — the comparison with Franco-Cantabria is a comparison of preservation, not cognition, and his caution cuts both ways: "elaborate Franco-Cantabrian cave art has no antecedents in Africa" — the two traditions, on current evidence, are independent inventions [S — Bednarik 2013]. What survives (the Apollo 11 therianthrope at the LGM, Qurta's bovids) proves the capacity existed continent-wide exactly when refugia concentrated people.

## 9.2 Music and the bone that maybe sang

The oldest instruments are flutes: at **Geissenklösterle** in the Swabian Jura, three flutes — two of swan bone, one of mammoth ivory — re-dated by Higham and colleagues to **42–43,000 years ago**, the oldest on record; the Hohle Fels vulture-radius flute (~40 ka, five finger holes, 21.8 cm preserved of ~34, an 8-mm bore) and Vogelherd fragments complete the Aurignacian ensemble [S — Conard and colleagues; the 2012 redating program]. Nicholas Conard's functional hypothesis deserves quotation in spirit: group bonding through music and dance may have been a strategic advantage of the first Europeans — social technology in acoustic form, in a world where network strength was survival.

The same Hohle Fels horizons have since yielded the earliest avian art — two thumbnail-size bird carvings, 1.3 and 0.7 grams (ptarmigan candidates), found 1.5 meters apart in a layer dated 2025–26 and argued to be the work of one hand [S-adjacent — Conard, Janas & Zeidi].

The counter-case is the Divje babe femur: a 43,000-year-old cave-bear femur with four holes (a fifth damaged) from a Slovenian cave, displayed at the National Museum of Slovenia as the world's oldest instrument — "the only one definitely made by Neanderthals," in the museum's framing — and, per the most careful analyses, almost certainly a carnivore's chew toy [C — the full docket]. For the flute: Ivan Turk's team (Kunej; Dimkaroski, whose replica does play a full scale, premiered on Slovenian television) — carnivore punctures are smooth and oval, these holes irregular and serrated; the thickest part of the diaphysis would shatter under a bite; metal-cast puncture experiments (Bastiani's method) reproduce the serration only with a chisel-punch; CT micro-traces argue human agency; the marrow cavity was cleaned. Against: d'Errico 1998 (matched punctures occur in ungnawed accumulations); Nowell & Chase 1998 (the bone is heavily gnawed, marrow present, both ends chewed); Morley 2006; and Diedrich's 2015 kill-shot across fifteen caves — every "pseudo-flute" in the record lacks stone-drill marks, the punctures match spotted-hyena premolars on cub femora, and stratigraphically the bones sit in Aurignacian, Gravettian, or bear-den contexts, not the Mousterian: "neither instruments nor human made at all" [S — *Royal Society Open Science*]. Alison Nowell's verdict stands as the field's: time to "dismantle a myth too persistent in some circles." The museum has not changed its label — institutional entrenchment, flagged in Part Ten's meta-lesson. The Neanderthal art case, if it survives the U-Th war, will not lean on this bone.

## 9.3 Fiber, thread, and dye: the invisible majority of technology

Almost everything ice-age people made has rotted, and the survival of a single string redraws the technological picture. At **Abri du Maras** in France's Rhône valley, a layer dated 41,000–52,000 years ago preserved a 6.2-millimeter cord of conifer bast, half a millimeter wide, three S-twist strands twined Z-direction, adhering to a six-centimeter flint flake [S — Hardy, Moncel, Kerfant et al., *Scientific Reports* 10:4889, 2020, cited 264; the 2022 redating program places the cord's layer in the site's older part, ~35–41 ka]. Its maker was a Neanderthal. The requirements cascade backward: harvesting in spring or early summer, when the sap rises (seasonal knowledge); retting the bark; beating and separating the fibers; and the twining itself — "cognitive understanding of numeracy and context-sensitive operational memory," in the authors' phrase. And once cordage exists, everything string makes possible exists by implication: bags, nets, mats, baskets, snares, lashings, structures, even watercraft. The previous oldest fiber was a soaked-flax fishing-net fragment from Ohalo II (~19 ka): Neanderthal string predates it by twenty millennia.

At **Dzudzuana** (Part Five), the LGM layer itself — the layer of the genomes — held 787 flax fibers, eighteen spun, one **knotted**, thirty-eight dyed in black, gray, turquoise, and a single pink, twenty-six thousand years before agriculture: mordanting or pigment-fixation knowledge inside the ice age's westernmost refuge [S — Kvavadze et al., *Science* 325:1359, 2009; the pre-LGM unit above it held 488 fibers with fifty-eight dyed and cut-marked]. At **Hohle Fels**, a 20-centimeter ivory baton refits from fifteen fragments, its four holes carrying spiral grooves, reproduces rope-making by the three-feeder, one-puller technique of pharaonic Egypt, dated 40–35 ka and verified experimentally by Veerle Rots of Liège: "the importance of rope and string for mobile hunters and gatherers trying to cope with the challenges of life in the Ice Age," in Conard's words [S — Conard & Rots, *Science Advances* 10:adh5217, 2024; funded by HeidelbergCement, the Baden-Württemberg ministry, and the Heidelberg Academy]. And at **Ohalo II** on the Sea of Galilee (~23 ka), waterlogged brushwood huts preserved twisted cords, notched net-sinkers, and the remains of fishing nets over a wild-grain processing floor — the ice age's lakeside village, kept for us by the rising water that ended it (Part Six).

## 9.4 Glue: fire, bark, and cumulative culture

Birch-bark tar, a hafting adhesive, requires heating bark in oxygen-starved conditions — and the Neanderthal tar from **Königsaue**, Germany (two pieces, 1.35 and 0.83 grams; layer A Micoquian, layer B Mousterian; dates bracketed 45–80 ka, with AMS minima of 43,800 and 48,400 and a documented phthalate-plasticizer contamination that once inflated ages by over four thousand years), carries the chemical fingerprint of the *hard* way: the suberin band in its infrared spectrum and its GC-MS profile match oxygen-restricted smoldering — underground or in an ash mound — not the simple above-ground condensation later shown to be feasible [S — Schmidt, Blessing, Rageot et al., *Archaeological and Anthropological Sciences* 15:76, 2023, cited 60; the Landesmuseum Halle holds the pieces, one preserving a Neanderthal fingerprint along with wood and stone-tool imprints]. The 2023 paper's verdict deserves quotation: this is "one of the clearest indicators of cumulative cultural evolution in the European Middle Palaeolithic" — a technique no single inventor could devise, demanding high-fidelity copying across generations of "cognitively opaque knowledge" (Csibra and Gergely's phrase for skills whose rationale the learner cannot reconstruct). The counter-arguments are kept in the record: Schmidt 2019 showed simple condensation *can* work, and Kozowyk's 2017 experimental program tested three production routes — tar is not per se a cognition proxy, though the actual Königsaue tar was made the difficult way; Villa's pine-resin-and-beeswax chemistry at two Italian caves and the observation that Neanderthals could make fire whenever they needed round out the technical file, with the Campitello tar claim (~200 ka, Mazza 2006) still unverified and flagged.

The uses extend beyond hafting. Experimental work shows tar mixed with holly-ash seals leather seams at eighteen times the lap-shear strength of plain tar — tailored, waterproof clothing without eyed needles — and 2026 work (Cologne/Oxford) proposes an antibacterial wound-treatment role, with Indigenous northern European and Canadian analogues [S-adjacent — Baker 2021; Siemssen 2026]. The flake dredged from the Doggerland seafloor with birch tar still on it (Part Six) is the same glue on the drowned plain: technology as portable as the people who carried it.

## 9.5 Ceramics: fired before farming

Pottery before agriculture remains one of the ice age's quiet revolutions, born independently at least twice under glacial stress — and both times, the first potters were mobile foragers, which overturns the old assumption that ceramics came with the sedentary grain economy. In East Asia the horizon is stunningly early: at **Xianrendong**, Jiangxi, shards dated **20–19 ka cal BP** — scorched cooking vessels of loess and powdered-mammoth-bone paste, built by sheet-laminating and coiling, fired low, ten millennia before farming [S — Wu, Zhang, Goldberg, Cohen, Pan, Arpin & Bar-Yosef, *Science* 336:1696, 2012, cited 542; wild rice phytoliths follow at Diaotonghuan ~12–9 ka]. Yuchanyan Cave in Hunan adds ~17.8 ka (Boaretto 2009). The tradition runs to the Amur basin (Gasya and Khummi, flat-bottomed vessels 16.2–14.1 ka) and to **Ōdai Yamamoto I** in Aomori — the world's oldest pottery *culture* (46 sherds, thirty from one vessel with carbonized residue, eight concordant AMS dates of ~14,500–14,000 BCE; plain undecorated ware; the people still nomadic, no permanent dwellings; the same horizon claims the world's oldest stone arrowheads; the Jomon sites' UNESCO listing came in 2021; the later Jomon carry a basal East Eurasian genome with the lowest Denisovan fraction of any sampled East Asians, Part Five) [S]. The European birth is independent and stranger: the Dolní Věstonice–Pavlov people fired clay figurines 28–24 ka in two kilns (the domed hearth with 2,300 fragments in situ; the horseshoe kiln) at 500–800°C, in a paste of loess and powdered mammoth bone that sintered through impurity-initiated liquid-phase glass formation — and **over half the kiln fragments show thermal-shock branching fractures: the figurines were made to explode in the firing**, ritual pyrotechnics, not craft failure [S — Vandiver, Soffer, Klima & Svoboda, *Science* 1989; the Gönnersdorf tradition continues to ~15 ka]. One Venus carries the fingerprint of a child, aged seven to fifteen, pressed into the surface before the fire (Králík, Novotný & Oliva's 2002–04 tomography); the ivory Venus XV face may be the ceramicist's portrait. Fired clay, like string and tar, arrived as cold-stress technology long before it served the Neolithic.

## 9.6 The dead and the beloved

Ice-age funerary practice is where a species' character shows most nakedly, and the record is now deep enough to compare across all the human lineages.

**Sapiens.** Sungir (Part Three): ten thousand hours of beadwork for bedridden children. Dolní Věstonice's triple burial (found 13 August 1986; 26,640±110 14C BP = 31,155±85 cal BP): three young men — confirmed all male by ancient DNA (Mittnik, Wang, Svoboda & Krause 2016, X-chromosome dosage and deaminated Y reads), which killed the 1990s narrative of a woman dead in childbirth — with DV15 placed first (severe congenital pathology: asymmetric limbs, deformed dentition and pelvis; the X-linked CCP diagnosis weakened by the male sexing), DV13 on his side facing him, a hand reaching toward DV15's pubic region, DV14 face-down; ochre on the heads; the pit covered by burnt spruce logs; grave goods of flint knives, Tertiary fossil-snail shells, pierced fox and wolf canines, and ivory headband pendants; DV14 and DV15 sharing identical mitochondrial DNA (maternal kin, plausibly brothers) and DV13 maternally unrelated; three rare shared traits (frontal-sinus aplasia, auditory exostoses, impacted wisdom teeth — all under five percent frequency; Alt et al.'s global comparison p=0.0007) supporting a family linkage, with the endogamy caveat attached; and the isolated human remains scattered across the site bearing carnivore processing — the cemetery held more dead than the graves preserve [S]. At Mal'ta, a three-year-old wore an ivory diadem. At Mezhirich, the porch drum. In Australia, Mungo Lady's cremation and Mungo Man's ochre burial (~40 ka) remain the oldest ritual funerals on Earth, in a landscape whose people later hid both back in the ground (Part Twelve). In the Americas, the Upward Sun River children lay under ochre with salmon (Part Seven). Everywhere: the dead were work.

**Neanderthals.** Their mortuary file is now long enough to be a pattern, not an anecdote, and it spans their entire range. Shanidar: the "flower burial" debunked — Solecki and Leroi-Gourhan's 1961–75 reading (pollen grains in anther-shaped clusters, seven of eight taxa medicinal) dissolved under Gargett's and then Sommer's critiques and the 2020s re-excavation's season test (pollen from flowers blooming at *different times of year* could not have been picked on one day; ground-nesting bees are the parsimonious vector; Hawks's surface-transect study had shown how insect-pollinated flowers accumulate naturally) — but the same campaign confirmed a deliberately scooped grave: "Shanidar Z," an articulated adult (the first in twenty-five years) laid in a hollowed depression at ~7 m depth, overlain by two horizontally placed stones (against the vertical rockfall fabric everywhere else), a chert blade-flake inside the curvature of the left first rib (lithics this size are vanishingly rare in the deposits), plant-tissue phosphatic cement around the body, a vertical limestone pillar at the head — "suspiciously like a modern gravestone," Emma Pomeroy's team allows — and the same spot reused (individuals 4, 6, 8, 9 cluster), a place of the dead [S — Pomeroy et al., *Antiquity* 2020; Hunt et al., *JAS* 2023; Hunt's pollen-and-wood finding inside Shanidar Z's own cavity closes the flower case for the new burial too]. Teshik-Tash (Uzbekistan; Okladnikov 1938–39): a child of eight to eleven in a shallow pit ringed by at least five pairs of Siberian ibex horn cores — the easternmost Neanderthal and its most famous alleged ritual, never formally refitted, no absolute dates [C]. Dederiyeh (Syria; Akazawa and Muhesen's team from 1987): two toddlers, each about two years old — Burial 1 (1993) on its back with a rectangular limestone slab at the head and a triangular flint near the heart; Burial 2 (1997–98) in a deliberately dug pit — from a site with a dozen individuals and a fifteen-paper Kyoto monograph; "the first face of a Neanderthal child." Mezmaiskaya (NW Caucasus; Golovanova from 1987): a neonate of about two weeks, flexed on its right side, head to the north, laid on a bed of 2–3 mm charcoal fragments concentrated around (none adjacent) the skeleton, ungnawed and calcite-cemented — buried at the very start of the occupation — and two later infants, one directly dated 39,700±1,100 BP, the genomed boy carrying late-European mitochondrial lineages: the Caucasus's own population turnover in one shelter (Part Eleven). Le Moustier 2 (Dordogne): the neonate found by Peyrony in 1914, "lost," and rediscovered by Maureille — France's own infant-burial file. The care file transcends burial: Shanidar 1 (35–50 years old; eleven documented pathologies — probable below-elbow amputation of the right arm with a decade of healed adaptation, osteomyelitic clavicle, crushed left orbit and probable blindness, an old scalp wound, grade-III auditory exostoses in *both* ears and probable deafness, degenerative knees and ankles) lived at least ten years on provisioning — "a one-armed, partially blind, crippled man could have made no pretense of hunting or gathering his own food," Trinkaus and Shipman wrote; "a testament to Neandertal compassion" — and the 2016 bioarchaeology-of-care analysis raises the possibility the arm was *amputated*, with bleeding controlled: surgery in a cave, and a possible teacher role for the old man [S].

**Violence and encounter.** Against that tenderness, the record keeps its darkness: Shanidar 3's projectile death (Churchill 2009: low kinetic energy, range weapon, probably sapiens-made, death days later); St-Césaire's survived blunt head trauma; Sima 16's multiple blows (deep-time intra-group violence); and the X-chromosome mating-pattern evidence of Part Eight. The encounters between human lineages were, at minimum, complicated — and the deserts in our genomes say most hybrid crossings failed to leave descendants.

## 9.7 Architecture, exchange, and the chemistry of trust

The built environment ran from brushwood huts through semi-subterranean bone houses to the twenty-tonne mammoth-bone mansions of Ukraine (Part Five) — Ohalo's six oval brushwood huts with their six hearths and one grave (twelve-plus rebuild episodes in Hut 1's concentric rings), Mal'ta–Buret's circular stone-lined dwellings with central hearths and storage pits, Mezhirich's engineering, Howick's 7,800-BC birch-and-turf house (Part Seven). The interpretation war over the whole class — dwelling, ritual house, seasonal base — runs through Soffer's "complex forager" model: construction effort of that scale implies multi-group cooperation beyond a single band.

Exchange networks supplied what refuges lacked, and their distances still embarrass our assumptions: the Baraba plain's people carried stone 1,000 km to the mammoth salt-licks of Volchia Griva (Part Four); Peña Capón's flint moved 700 km through at least six bands' worth of hands (Part Four); Mumba's obsidian crossed 320 km from the Naivasha basin (Merrick & Brown's sourcing); the Mezhirich porch held amber from 350–500 km away; the Volgu cache documents 200 km transport of Solutrean laurel-leaf points (Part Ten); Shiyu's obsidian came from 800–1,000 km; the Caucasus Micoquian carried Zayukovo obsidian 250 km (Doronicheva); and Sungir's ochre-stuffed femur belonged to a person of a different region entirely. The chemistry of these networks is generosity with accounting. Ambrose's description of the !Kung hxaro gift system is the ethnographic key: "It's free! It's yours! And don't you forget it!" — a gift is a claim. Down-the-line trade converts strangers into insurance; the ethnographic ceiling (Baffinland Inuit annual ranges ~25,000 km²) makes Peña Capón's 88,995 km² network the largest known forager system on Earth. The mathematics is survival engineering, not sentimentality: a maximal-band safety net spanning an area the size of Austria is what a 130,000-person continent can afford — and when the safety net held (HS2, the LGM), people lived; when it broke (late Gravettian), the archaeological record goes thin.

## 9.8 Food: the larder and its end

Under the ice, people ate the steppe: horse, reindeer, bison, mammoth, and — as big game thinned near the glacial front — the small-game shift (hare, rabbit, marmot, birds) that the Venus barometer records (Part Three). The collapse of that larder (Part Four) forced the broad-spectrum adaptations of the deglaciation: intensified shellfish on the Atlantic facade (El Mazo, Part Six), lakeside fishing (Ohalo), wild-grain processing that pre-figures everything Neolithic. The Ohalo campers, tellingly, selected adult ungulates preferentially — a rich littoral niche chosen, not a starving fallback; abundance, skillfully used, was the ice-age norm wherever a refuge held. The Willandra people were grinding wild grass on stones before 18,000 years ago, contemporary with the Middle East's first grain experiments: the same subsistence logic, an ocean apart.

In New Guinea's highlands — a landscape people had occupied for fifty millennia, managing fire in every major valley (Telefomin, Haeapugua, Wahgi) — foragers turned management into cultivation on the Kuk Swamp's post-LGM alluvial fan: phase 1, wetland-margin pits, stake-holes, and runnels by 10,220–9,910 cal BP, extending cultivation into a drier phase; phase 2, mounded gardening by 6,950–6,440 cal BP with deliberate planting of Eumusa bananas and taro starch on tools; phase 3, full ditched field systems by 4,350–3,980 cal BP [S — Denham et al., *Science* 301:189, 2003; Haberle's charcoal curves show valley-wide clearance from ~7.8 ka; Roberts 2017 documents the terminal-Pleistocene foraging baseline]. It is one of three natural experiments in what a post-glacial larder can become — the Fertile Crescent's stress-driven farming, New Guinea's plenty-managed-into-gardens, and Amazonia's empty-LGM forest co-created by its late-arriving people (Part Ten) — three answers to the same question the melting ice asked every refuge: *now what do you eat?*

## 9.9 The archives themselves were refugees

One final custodial fact: the evidence in this document survives by accident, and it is still being lost. Předmostí, one of the richest Gravettian cemeteries on Earth, burned with Mikulov Castle in 1945 — several dozen individuals survive as casts. Kisese II's excavation notes were stolen from Raymond Inskeep's home in the 1990s. The Apollo 11 Cave was vandalized in 2007 and left unprotected. Star Carr's waterlogged bone is dissolving: 2000s field drainage dropped the water table, pyrite in the sediment oxidized to sulfuric acid at pH 2, and bone excavated as 'incredibly robust' in 1948 now arrives in the laboratory as demineralized 'jelly' — an archive dying within a single generation of its rediscovery [S — Milner et al. 2011; 2023–24 conservation]. Brazil's National Museum fire of 2018 consumed the context of the Luzia skull; some 80% of her fragments survived in a metal box, and reassembly continues. Volchia Griva's Soviet-era pavilion was dismantled and its exhibits lost; Berelekh's bone bed was looted out by modern ivory hunters — 'this is the end of the story,' its excavator wrote. Even the institutional solutions decayed: Mungo's double-key vault dissolved when the state dismissed its management structures in 2013. The ice age's record is not a monument. It is a refugee camp of perishable things, guarded — when it is guarded at all — by people who inherited it mid-catastrophe.

# PART TEN — THE FIGHTS: THIS FIELD'S PERMANENT CONTROVERSIES

Every science has its disputed ground; ice-age studies are mostly disputed ground. The fights matter to the reader because they are not failures of the field — they are its immune system. This part gathers the document's running controversies into one place, adds the two that did not fit elsewhere, and closes with the meta-lesson: how to weigh a claim in a science built on bones, dirt, and statistics.

## 10.1 The Solutrean hypothesis: the Atlantic crossing that wasn't

The most persistent fringe claim in American archaeology holds that Solutrean people of southwest Europe (~25–23 ka) — whose laurel-leaf points, made by pressure-flaking and heat treatment, were the most sophisticated lithic technology on Earth (the Volgu cache documents their long-distance transport over 200 km) — crossed the North Atlantic pack ice and founded Clovis. Dennis Stanford and Bruce Bradley built the case from 2004 (the *Current Anthropology* proposal) through their 2012 book *Across Atlantic Ice*: biface similarity (overshot flaking), the Cinmar biface dredged with a mastodon off Virginia, Delmarva Peninsula surface finds (Darrin Lowery's collections), and mitochondrial haplogroup X2a [C — the full docket].

The kill-file is long and peer-reviewed [S]. On X2a: the 2008 *AJHG* analysis (the Brazilian team) showed it nests within the single Native American founding pool; the 2012 *AJPA* paper showed haplogroup C4c — indubitably Asian — has a parallel history to X2a, collapsing the special pleading; Kennewick Man carried basal X2a with zero European signal, and the deepest X2a branches sit on the *west coast*. On the autosomes: the Mal'ta genome explained the west-Eurasian affinity in Native Americans as arriving *via Siberia* (shared ANE ancestry, not a transatlantic voyage; Lazaridis 2014 put ANE into both Europeans and Native Americans — common descent, not direct migration). On the archaeology: the 2015 *American Antiquity* report showed the celebrated Delmarva bipoints are visually indistinguishable from Holocene bipoints all along the eastern seaboard — their claimed rarity and pre-LGM-only distribution cannot be sustained; there are no boats, no representations of boats, no settlement evidence (Straus: "no evidence whatsoever of seafaring"), a 6,000–8,000-year gap between Solutrean and Clovis that no technology bridges; the oceanography fails (Westley & Dix 2008); and even the hypothesis's last quantitative argument — Oppenheimer, Bradley & Stanford's 2014 *World Archaeology* "mammoth in the room" paper — relies on an east-Asian-fraction reading that mainstream analyses attribute to dilution. David Meltzer's verdict is the field's summary: the hypothesis requires "cultural amnesia, genetic amnesia, dental amnesia, linguistic amnesia, and skeletal amnesia."

Two reasons the case still instructs. First, its sociology: the claim was adopted by white-nationalist circles as "Europeans were first" (a separate figure, Ray Hranicky, runs a parallel volunteer flintknapping circuit, flagged as not-to-be-conflated), and the 2018 CBC documentary *The Ice Bridge* drew open condemnation from archaeologists — Jennifer Raff's widely read critique documented the platforming problem — with Bradley and Stanford protesting their innocence, pointing to Huron-Wendat participation (Louis Lesage). It is the documented case study in how a falsified archaeological claim gets weaponized. Second, its afterlife: **Parsons Island**, Maryland, where Lowery's leaf-shaped tool from an LGM-dated dark layer generated 2024–25 headlines (286 artifacts over ninety-three visits; a "complete matching Solutrean toolkit" in the press; Stanford's kelp-and-Atlantic comment) — but Lowery's own admission of downslope transport puts the true age at 15,000–20,500 BP, and as of September 2026 no peer-reviewed publication exists. Grade M until published; the watch item is named.

## 10.2 Monte Verde: the keystone under fire

Everything written above about the Americas rests, in the public imagination, on one site: Monte Verde, in southern Chile — the 14,500-year-old camp that broke the Clovis-first orthodoxy when Tom Dillehay's decades of excavation (the 1970s–90s, with the waterlogged MV-II layer of wooden structures, hearths, and *kelp*-bearing food remains) convinced a skeptical profession in 1997. In March 2026 the keystone was attacked directly [C — actively litigated]. Todd Surovell and colleagues, in *Science*, reported the first independent reinvestigation in fifty years — enabled by a fact that itself became evidence: the original excavations were destroyed by flooding decades ago, and Dillehay had held exclusive permits throughout, so no outsider could check. From nine new exposures in the Chinchihuapi Creek valley, new radiocarbon and luminescence work, and the Lepué Tephra (11,000 BP) found stratigraphically *underlying* the archaeological component across an erosional contact, they argued the famous layer is reworked and no older than the Middle Holocene — 8,200–4,200 cal BP — with Pleistocene wood and organics redeposited from older sediments. Their conclusion: the colonization of South America is "more recent than anchored by Monte Verde."

The defense is already in the field: Dillehay and thirty-plus colleagues published scathing critiques, and a counter-study ("Monte Verde II: an assessment of new radiocarbon dates and their sedimentological context") reports forty-three new radiocarbon dates reaffirming ~14,550 cal BP — with the core criticism that the attack extrapolates from exposures far from the destroyed site itself. The investigator pattern deserves note: Surovell is the same researcher who led the failed 2009 multi-lab replication of Younger Dryas impact "spherules" and the 2011 PIDBA demographic paper — serial establishment-challenger is a real role in this field, sometimes right, sometimes not. And the stakes are survivable: even if Monte Verde fell, pre-Clovis would stand on Cooper's Ferry, Paisley, Gault, Huaca Prieta (~15 ka), Pali Aike, and Pilauco (12.5–11 ka). But the keystone would leave a hole, the kelp highway would lose its anchor site, and the referee class would have its biggest trophy. As of September 2026: standing, contested, and watched.

## 10.3 The Younger Dryas impact hypothesis: anatomy of a collapse

The claim: a comet exploded over North America ~12,900 years ago, triggering the Younger Dryas cold snap and the megafauna extinction — a hypothesis (Firestone et al. 2007) whose fifteen-year run is now a masterclass in how a beautiful catastrophe dies [S — the rebuttals, one by one]. The platinum: the celebrated Greenland spike dates **forty-five years *after*** the cold snap began and lasted fourteen years — Icelandic fissure volcanism, not impact (Green et al. 2025). The crater: Hiawatha, once the hypothesis's crown jewel, is 58 million years old (Late Paleocene, per its shocked zircon). The nanodiamonds: refuted by Daulton's materials analysis and Melosh's pressure-physics calculations — the claimed formation mechanism is physically impossible at the proposed sites. The black mats: occur at any-age sites across the Atacama (Pigati) and in multi-site lake-sediment records (Marlon) with no pattern at 12.9 ka. The spherules: Surovell's 2009 multi-lab replication found none. The genetics: **no bottleneck exists in any ancient DNA set at the event.** The human element: the hypothesis's public face, "Allen West," is an alias of Alan Whitt, convicted in California for fraudulently masquerading as a licensed geologist; several co-authors have distanced themselves on the record, and the unified rebuttal (Pinter, Boslough, Melosh and colleagues) documents contamination of a key sample with modern carbon. What survives from the episode is demographic, not cosmic: archaeological summed-probability curves genuinely do show a population decrease or settlement reorganization across North America at the Younger Dryas onset (Surovell et al. 2011, the PIDBA database), with similar declines across much of the Northern Hemisphere except the Middle East; and Iberia's SPD shows growth through the Bølling-Allerød, then a rapid decrease at the Younger Dryas onset (13.5–12.7 ka) and a lengthy stagnation — Iberia, notably, did **not** act as a Younger Dryas refugium, matching the ancient-DNA turnover of ~14.5 ka [S — Fernández-López de Pablo et al., *Scientific Reports* 2019, PMC6478856; recovery only from 10.2–8 ka]. The humans felt the cold snap. The sky had nothing to do with it.

## 10.4 The linguistic refugia: Nichols' framework and its abuses

Johanna Nichols' *Linguistic Diversity in Space and Time* (1992) gave the refugium idea its rigorous linguistic form. **Spread zones** — the steppes, later western Europe — have low diversity, repeated long-range spreads, shallow time depth, and languages similar to one another. **Residual (accretion) zones** — mountains, coasts, islands: the Caucasus, New Guinea, northern Australia, the Pacific rim — accumulate old, deeply diversified families and isolates, correlate with head-marking typology, and function as "a linguistic refugium of sorts," with the crucial caveat that **languages are drawn to such zones, not pushed**: residual does not mean refugee. Colin Renfrew's farming-dispersal "punctuations" supplied the spread-zone engine, and the method's ceiling is hard: standard historical linguistics reliably reaches five to six thousand years, and everything beyond that courts analogy.

The cautionary tale is the **Vasconic hypothesis** — Theo Vennemann's reconstruction of a Basque-like family (Basque, Aquitanian, Ligurian, Iberian, Proto-Sardinian) spreading from an Aquitanian ice-age refuge, argued from river-name persistence ("the toponymic main axiom": names outlast their speakers), claimed cognates (*saiwiz/itsaso* 'sea', *īsą/izotz* 'ice', *Eber-* ~ *ibar* 'valley', Val d'Aran/Arendal ~ *haran*), Gimbutas-era cultural parallels, and vigesimal counting, collected in *Europa Vasconica – Europa Semitica* (2003). The demolition is named and specific: Kitson (the hydronymy is Indo-European after all), Baldi & Richard, Meier-Brügger, Steinbauer ("insurmountable obstacles"), Lakarra (Proto-Basque phonology won't bear it), Trask (an agglutinating non-Basque substratum instead), Bichlmeier, Udolph (*muna* is Indo-European; Proto-Basque lacked initial /m/), Georg, Reichardt, Sheynin, Gąsiorowski ("Old European Hydronymy may not exist"), Kayser & Roewer 2013 (genetics cannot tell language), Bauer 2004 (vigesimal systems have Indo-European explanations) — and Anreiter's method parody, re-"translating" Vennemann's Vasconic river names into Turkish. Supporters (Dieterlen & Bengtson 2017) and a Greek-"Pelasgian" loanword list (community-grade, flagged) keep the flame; the telling detail is the sociology — Stephen Oppenheimer speaks at Vasconic conferences while co-authoring fringe-population claims, the Oxford beachcomber school and the Munich Vasconic school interlocking, while genetics shows Basques carry majority post-Mesolithic (steppe-era) ancestry: a continent-wide Mesolithic Vasconic cannot survive two replacements. What survives is the small-survival case: Basque as Europe's most plausible single-remnant language, exactly as the maternal-lineage data frame it (Part Five). The defensible core, per Nichols herself: refugium logic applied to linguistics is a map of *where old things persist*, and a temptation that keeps over-extending — the Amazon refugia hypothesis (extended from birds to plants to *languages* by Meggers and colleagues, and collapsed when the pollen record spoke) is the template for how far is too far.

The rigorous applications are worth naming: the one accretion-zone analysis in the Americas (a 2024 *American Journal of Physical Anthropology* study using founder-effect logic) locates the earliest language diversity of the first Americans in the Lower Mississippi [S]; and the **Dene-Yeniseian** link — Edward Vajda's Ket–Na-Dene demonstration at roughly 4–6 ka, attached to the Syalakh-Belkachi populations of eastern Siberia (Part Seven) — is the deepest claimed relationship on Earth, still standing under specialist fire.

## 10.5 Amazonia: the empty glacial savannah and the co-created forest

The refugium debate's longest-running theater is Amazonia, and its resolution inverts the classic script. Haffer's 1969 Pleistocene forest refugia — proposed for birds, extended to plants, lizards (Vanzolini), and, in Betty Meggers's work, *languages* (language diversity as corroboration) — largely collapsed when pollen cores showed forest persisting through the dry phases [S — "What has become of the refugia hypothesis," PMC6468052]. The human record completes the reversal: during the LGM, southwestern Amazonia (the Noel Kempff Mecardo transect, Bolivian ecotone; Maezumi, Power and colleagues) was expansive C4 savanna-woodland with remarkably *low* fire (challenging the fire-plus-CO2 maintenance models), a sedimentary hiatus at 18–11 ka, and a human presence between 19,500 and 11,000 years ago that was minimal to absent; systematic occupation begins ~10,500 years ago, tracking the forest's return — and then humans became the forest's co-authors. The signatures: domesticated tree species growing five times denser near archaeological sites (Levis et al., *Science* 2017); hundreds of managed crop lineages with the southwest as a domestication center (Clement's 83–85 species); riverine-bluff settlement corridors; raised fields and geoglyphs (Watling's 4,000-year earthworks); and pre-1500 populations plausibly of eight to twenty million — against McMichael's sobering audit that **less than 0.0005 percent of the forest has been archaeologically sampled**, with ecological plots biased toward ancient habitation (regenerating forests absorb more carbon, so even the carbon sink is overestimated). The "pristine wilderness" is the artifact of post-Columbian population collapse (Neves's two-mode model): the forest people returned to made the forest that the world then mistook for untouched.

The Lagoa Santa record preserves the first South Americans — and the debate their skulls provoked. Ferraz et al. 2023 (*Nature Ecology & Evolution*; thirty-four genomes from eight sites, ≥10 ka, FAPESP-funded with Tübingen and Senckenberg) settled the two-wave question: **Luzio is a full Amerindian**, a descendant of the single founding wave (≤16 ka), and the "Paleoamerican" morphology is not a separate population. The sambaqui shell-mound builders — the "kings of the coast," in the excavators' phrase, among the highest demographic densities in pre-colonial South America — vanished ~2 ka ago; Lapa do Santo's burial program (the oldest decapitated and zigzag-engraved burials ~9.6 ka) carries mtDNA A2/B2/C1d1/D4h3a and Y Q1a2a plus rare C2b (whose presence disproves the late-arrival hypothesis once attached to it); one early individual still carries the ancestral EDAR allele; LS18 clusters with Anzick-1. The 2026 endocranial study (López-Sosa et al.) finds Lagoa Santa endocasts clustering closest to Nubians, then Australians — the "Two Main Biological Components Model" survives only as *retention of plesiomorphic morphology under isolation*, the same reading as Iwo Eleru, Kow Swamp, and WLH50; only the geologically oldest Lagoa Santa DNA-sampled individual (Sumidouro) carries the Australasian signal. Material loss frames it: the Luzia skull — Richard Neave's facial reconstruction once given African features under the dead theory — lost 80 percent of its fragments to the 2018 National Museum fire; the metal box that held the skull survived, and reassembly of the fragments continues (Brazil's Předmostí moment: twenty million objects, Part Nine's archive file).

## 10.6 The fringe-drowned-worlds: Mu, Lemuria, Atlantis-in-Doggerland, and the real Mauritia

Every drowned landscape in this document has acquired a mythic double, and the doubles follow a strict law [M — documented]: they parasitize real discoveries one cycle behind. **Lemuria** began as a scientific hypothesis — Philip Sclater's 1864 attempt to explain lemur biogeography across the Indian Ocean ( Quarterly Journal of Science) — was adopted by Ernst Haeckel in 1870 as the cradle of humanity, then bloomed into the occult: Blavatsky's 1888 *Secret Doctrine* made it the home of the third root race (egg-laying hermaphrodites with a third eye), James Churchward's 1926 *Lost Continent of Mu* built a Pacific super-civilization on a Maya inscription misreading (long since corrected by decipherment), Tamil nationalism raised **Kumari Kandam** from the same seed, and Steiner's anthroposophy, the Mount Shasta "Lemurian" cult, and a 1972 claim of a Maui-Oahu sunken city censored by U.S. Naval Intelligence complete the genealogy. Lemuria died when the theory it explained was born — plate tectonics made the lost bridge unnecessary.

The **Atlantis-in-Doggerland** genre is younger and better fed: Langdon's 2025 "Atlantis Found" fits Plato's dimensions to the Dogger Bank at R²=0.998 (Plato's plain 370×550 km; the bank 375×560; *nēsos* read as "peninsula"), maps the "ringed cities" onto British causewayed enclosures with hydrologically recalibrated dates (Windmill Hill 6400 BCE, Hambledon Hill 6100, Whitehawk 6000, Camp de César 5600, Champ Durand 5400), reads Stonehenge's Slaughter Stone as a relief map of Doggerland, redeposited megafauna as Plato's elephants ("the Subtropical Britain Hoax"), and tabulates a megalith-diaspora exodus (Brittany 4100–3900 BCE, Iberia 3800–3500, Malta and Sardinia 3400–3100) — a numerological retrofit that ignores radiocarbon calibration and never says what would disprove it; a 2026 trade book (*The Mystery of Doggerland: Atlantis in the North Sea*) re-heats Storegga with a dubious "warmer than today until 1200 BCE" claim. And the instructive exception: **Mauritia is real** — zircon crystals three billion years old in the two-million-year-old basalts of Mauritius betray a microcontinent the size of Japan that sank ~84 million years ago during the breakup of Gondwana [S — Torsvik et al., *Nature Geoscience* 2013]. A genuine Atlantis-class event, 84 million years too early for any witness.

The deconstruction axiom for the reader, stated once and applied throughout: when a drowned-continent claim appears, ask which real discovery it is feeding on, whether it makes testable predictions, and whether its author ever says what would prove it wrong. Rose's Gulf Oasis passes the first two tests and awaits the third (Part Six); Langdon's Atlantis fails all three; Mauritia passed every test except the one that mattered to the mythmakers — being inhabited at all.

## 10.7 How to weigh a claim: the meta-lesson

Across twenty-four research dives, a pattern repeats often enough to be a law of this science, and it is stated here as the reader's toolkit:

1. **Single fossils move, and are moved, less than headlines suggest.** Misliya shifted the consensus (multiple independent dating methods, diagnostic morphology, sealed context, bedding, hunting economy); Apidima has not (one bone, a dating spread from >300 ka to <40 ka on the same sample, no context, named heavyweight critics). The difference is evidentiary plurality, not charisma.
2. **Methods have failure modes, and mature fields publish them.** U-Th's inversions sit in the caves' own published tables; FitCoal's structural blind spot was found by the people who built better structure models; OSL single-grain work deflated Jebel Faya's 125 ka claim from within; the cattle-glue and phthalate contaminations are textbook cases. The correction cycle — claim, critique, replication, synthesis — is not scandal; it is the engine, and its speed is the field's health.
3. **Adversarial refereeing works.** Every American pre-Clovis anchor survives *because* someone professional tried to kill it: Fiedel across Paisley, Cooper's Ferry, and the Delmarva claims; Chatters at Chiquihuite; Surovell at the impact hypothesis and now Monte Verde; Stringer against the 930 ka bottleneck; the Cobbold-1000 signatories against ancient-DNA over-reach; Bednarik against art-history exceptionalism; Tzedakis against cryptic refugia. The referee class deserves the same respect as the discoverers, and Part Thirteen names them.
4. **Watch who benefits.** Permit monopolies (Monte Verde's fifty-year exclusivity, now central to the 2026 attack); museum entrenchment (Divje babe's unchanging label); fast-track publication (Ryan–Pitman's one-day acceptance); and political adoption (the Solutrean hypothesis's nationalist afterlife; the Sámi indigeneity inversion, both sides; Jebel Faya's UNESCO listing despite the species-neutral revision). Who benefits is data about a claim, not just about people.

With that toolkit, the reader is equipped for everything this document contains — and for the next headline.

## 11.1 Neanderthals: the northern veterans

They were the ice age's senior citizens — a lineage that emerged in Europe's Middle Pleistocene cold (its evolution tracks MIS 11 and the later Middle Pleistocene) and endured half a dozen glacial cycles before we arrived [S]. Their genome records the apprenticeship: low heterozygosity (1.6 differences per 10,000 bases in the high-coverage Vindija genome — thinner than any modern population), long runs of shared DNA in small communities (Chagyrskaya: an isolated group of fewer than sixty, hunting bison by following their seasonal migrations, juvenile females preferred), and repeated population turnovers — the 2018 hypochlorite-decontamination method unlocked five late Neanderthals (39–47 ka) and exposed a *late turnover*: eastern early lineages replaced by western-European-related late Neanderthals, visible in the Caucasus too, where the 44.6-ka Mezmaiskaya boy carries late-European mitochondrial lineages while his site's earlier infants carry the local NM2 clade (Part Nine's mortuary file).

Their deep history hides stranger things. The 430,000-year-old people of Sima de los Huesos, Spain — already on the Neanderthal line — carried Denisovan-like *mitochondrial* DNA, implying either an early Denisovan-range connection or mitochondrial capture across a hybridizing population; and modern-human DNA introgressed *into* Neanderthals 200–250 thousand years ago at 5–10 percent of the Altai genome (Part Eight) — our ancestors were inside the Neanderthal gene pool before the Neanderthals were inside ours. The two interbreeding episodes, ~200,000 years apart, both left the same sex-biased signature (Part Eight).

Their material culture under cold stress achieved genuine complexity: birch-tar adhesive by the hard, cumulative route (Part Nine); fiber string at Abri du Maras; the only Middle Palaeolithic mammoth-bone structure at Molodova (Part Five); the Chatelperronian borderland ornamentation (contested as imitation); possible cave art — La Pasiega's 64.8 ka ladder, Ardales' 65.5 ka flowstone-pierced stalagmite, Maltravieso's 66.7 ka hand stencil — pending the U-Th war (Part Nine); and at Gorham's Cave, engravings older than 39,000 years on a floor that outlasted the species. Gibraltar's promontory, with its drowned five-kilometer coastal plain and buffered climate, plausibly the last Neanderthal landscape on Earth [C on the late dates — claims to 28 and even 24 ka contested; the mainstream last Neanderthals are ~40–42 ka elsewhere, with Iberian late-survival arguments unresolved].

Their end is a map, not a moment: gone from the southern Caucasus by ~40 ka (Pinhasi's synthesis; Bondi Cave's sterile layer ~35.4 ka seals the sequence), holding pockets of Iberia possibly later, Altai communities persisting to ~45–40 ka — where Chagyrskaya's genomic paper records *two* separate dispersals into southern Siberia (an eastern-European Micoquian-related movement ~60 ka and the earlier Altai lineage), with least-cost modeling suggesting the Caucasus–Altai connection ran north of the Caspian in 2,000-year pulses during MIS 5e or MIS 3 (Coco & Iovita 2025), across exactly the terrain where Denisovans lived. Caucasus forensics add the dynastic detail: the early Mezmaiskaya population forms its own mitochondrial clade (NM2, sister to the Polish Stajnia line NM1 — the same early branch that holds the Iberian Forbes' Quarry and Galería de las Estatuas fossils, 100–130 ka), an infant's tooth preserves the first documented Neanderthal mitochondrial *heteroplasmy* (Andreeva et al. 2022), and the region's Neanderthals resolve into two culturally distinct populations — an eastern-Micoquian west and a Zagros-linked east (Doronicheva 2023). Their bequest to the survivors — 1–4 percent of every non-African genome, immune and altitude adaptations, the lesson of the burial and the tar — is the ice age's most literal inheritance; and roughly 40% of the Neanderthal genome is recoverable, reassembled, from living people. The missing 60% is the measure of what small, repeated bottlenecks erase.

## 11.2 Denisovans: the empire without a face

For a decade the Denisovans were a genome without a fossil: the 2010 finger bone from Denisova Cave (Krause et al.) whose mitochondrial DNA revealed a lineage sister to Neanderthals, named for the cave because there was nothing else to name; Matthias Meyer's single-stranded library delivered the 30x genome in 2012 [S]. The empire has since been mapped by its genes and its scattered bones. The cave itself: Denisova 3 (the finger), Denisova 4 (a toe — Neanderthal, 2014, proving both species used the cave), Denisova 8 (a deeper-diverging molar), Denisova 11 ("Denny," the F1 hybrid: Neanderthal mother, Denisovan father, Slon et al. 2018), and sediment DNA reading the layers directly. Beyond it: the Baishiya mandible (160 ka, proteins only; the 48–32 ka rib; sedaDNA at 100–60 ka), the Cobra Cave child's molar (164–131 ka, Laos; warm-adapted; identified by proteins matching Xiahe), the Harbin cranium's Denisovan signals (146 ka), and a range that — on genomic evidence — spanned at least three deeply divergent populations [S — Jacobs et al., *Cell* 177:1010, 2019: 161 genomes, 14 island groups; D1 and D2 separated by >350,000 years; one Denisovan group's introgression continued east of the Wallace Line until near the end of the Pleistocene].

They were the archipelago's other people. The D1 pulse into Sahul's ancestors ~46 ka, the D2 pulse ~25–31 ka after arrival, the Philippines' independent D3 pulse reaching ~5% in the Ayta (the world's highest fractions; Larena et al. 2021), the super-archaic (*erectus*-grade) ancestry reaching Oceanians through Denisovan intermediaries (TRACE 2026; Roger 2020's two-event history), and the separate Altai-like D0 pulse in East Asians (Browning 2018): the Denisovans were not one cave in Siberia but a Southeast Asian metapopulation — one that may include, or overlap, *Homo luzonensis* (below), with Reilly et al. 2026 finding three Denisovan-like sources in Near Oceanians and the method wars (S′, f4, ArchIE, IBDMix; Peede et al.'s 2026 review) honestly catalogued. And they held the highest ground: when sapiens could not yet live year-round on the Tibetan Plateau, Denisovans did — for over a hundred thousand years, through glacial and interglacial alike (Part Five). The EPAS1 altitude allele that lets Tibetans live at 4,000 meters today is theirs, transmitted through lowland East Asians 48–46 thousand years ago, selected only later. We inherited their mountain long after they were gone.

"Denny" remains their story's single most human page: a first-generation child of two species, found in a cave both her peoples called home — the encounters made flesh.

## 11.3 The island relicts: hobbits and the Callao people

Two island lineages bracketed the sapiens crossing to Sahul, both descended from *erectus*-grade ancestors, both isolated for the better part of a million years, both gone before or just as we arrived.

On **Flores**, the ancestors at Mata Menge (~700 ka) shrank within some 300,000 years from erectus stature to the meter-tall *Homo floresiensis* — island dwarfing, the same physics that miniaturized the stegodons they hunted — with a tool record back to 1.02 Ma at Wolo Sege. Their end, per the 2025 stalagmite record, was climate, not us: rainfall collapsed by 37% between 76 and 61 ka, severe summer drought set in by 61–55 ka, ninety-two percent of their remains fall between 76 and 62 ka, the stegodons crashed first, the hobbits abandoned Liang Bua, a major volcanic eruption followed ~50 ka, and modern humans appear at the cave only ~46,000 years ago. The island had already buried its first refugees before the second wave landed; no floresiensis DNA survives in the modern Flores pygmies (Gagan et al. 2025; Kaifu 2024; the Liang Bua team's careful "probably accidental rafting" for every earlier crossing).

On **Luzon**, *Homo luzonensis* — the Callao Cave teeth, finger and toe bones of at least three individuals, a mosaic of Australopithecus-like and modern features, revised to ≥134 ka (Grün 2023) — required open-sea crossings to an island never connected: hominins were rafting to the Philippines 709,000 years ago (the butchered rhino of Kalinga), and something human persisted there until our ancestors arrived. Whether luzonensis *is* the source of the Ayta's D3 Denisovan pulse is unresolved; the possibility that the archipelago's Denisovans wore multiple faces is live.

And beneath both, the deep-time background: Flores colonized 1.02 million years ago; Timor's Laili Cave holding 44,600-year-old sapiens — the oldest in Wallacea — with Lene Hara at 30–35 ka, Matja Kuru's savanna hunters (40–21 ka, isotopes tracking the open country until the terminal-Pleistocene forest closed), and Jerimalai's pelagic deep-sea tuna at 42 ka (O'Connor et al., *Science* 2011 — deliberate offshore fishing, forty-two thousand years before agriculture); Sulawesi's Liang Bua sequence preserves woven technology (133 edge-glossed tools). Every gap our ancestors crossed to reach Sahul had been crossed before, by someone else. The archipelago was an archaic graveyard that became a sapiens highway — and its drowned floor (Part Five) almost certainly still holds the missing bones.

## 11.4 The encounters: how the species met

The meetings are the ice age's least documented and most consequential events. The genomic ledger records the outcomes without the scenes: admixture at 47 ka and 44–54 ka and 31 ka; a sex bias toward Neanderthal-male pairings persisting across two episodes 200,000 years apart; deserts purged in a hundred generations; Denisovan pulses arriving before and after the Sahul landing; a ghost lineage in everyone at 0.83 million years; "Denny," a child of both species, real. The archaeological ledger adds texture — Shanidar 3's projectile wound, the Châtelperronian borderland of ornament and ambiguity, the possible final Gibraltar overlap, the Divje babe dispute, the La Pasiega authorship question hanging on the U-Th war — but the meetings themselves left no diaries, and this document will not invent any.

What can be said honestly: the encounters lasted millennia, spanned at least two continents and two episodes 200,000 years apart, produced children, and ended with one species inheriting the others' genes, altitude adaptations, and adhesive recipes — and, so far as anyone can tell, none of their languages. The last word belongs to the one datum both genomes agree on: the extinct lineages persist in the survivors. The ice age's final integration was genetic.

## 12.1 Flood memory: two families of stories

The drowned lands of Part Six left no writing except in one place — the river civilizations of Mesopotamia, whose flood literature is the only ice-age flood memory preserved by the displaced themselves. The corpus has a genealogy. The Old Babylonian **Atrahasis** epic (the seven-days-and-nights variant). The Standard Babylonian **Gilgamesh**, Tablet XI: Utnapishtim's account — Ea's warning whispered through the reed wall, the boat of equal dimensions, the six days and nights of flood, the grounding on Mount Nisir, the dove, swallow, and raven, the sacrifice, and the gods' translation of the survivors "to the mouth of the rivers." And **Genesis 6–9**, which follows the same scaffold with its own variations (three decks against six; the raven and three doves; Ararat against Nisir, three hundred miles apart; blessing against deification) — the direction of borrowing running through the Babylonian exile, in Tigay's and Hendel's line-by-line demonstrations [S — the comparative literature is a century old].

Around the core: Greek **Deucalion** (Prometheus' son; the chest of fifty by fourteen by eight cubits in Apollodorus; nine days of flood; Parnassus, with Etna, Athos, and Othrys as rival landings; the "bones of our mother" read as stones that became people; the Parian Chronicle's date of 1528 BC; and Lucian's Syrian variant, with animal pairs and a pilgrimage temple at Manbij where "all the waters were swallowed"). Indian **Matsya and Manu**: the horn-towed fish, the seven sages, the Malaya mountains — a *cyclical* flood framed by kalpa and manvantara time, renewal rather than punishment, Vishnu's first avatar overlaid on the older fish-helper tale (Śatapatha Brāhmana's fourth-century BCE layer). Chinese **Gun-Yu**: flood *control* — Yu the engineer taming the waters by labor and channels, the functional inverse of the ark. And the Hopi cycle: Waynaboozhoo's raft, the animal swimmers, the turtle-island earth.

The archaeological correlate sharpens the corpus into two families. **Family one — the drowning world** (Mesopotamia, Genesis, Greece, Hopi): displacement memory of basin transgressions, peaking where Holocene rise drowned great river lowlands — the Gulf Oasis above all, where sixty-plus sites appear "overnight" ~7,500 years ago on a shoreline that was Great-Britain-sized land within human memory, and where the flood myth's content and structure stay consistent from 2,500 BC through Genesis to the Qur'an (Rose's observation, Part Six) [C — the hypothesis is untested; the literary bridge is real regardless of where the water was]. **Family two — recession and return** (Australia above all): twenty-one Aboriginal stories from sixteen coastal locations encode postglacial sea-level events with uncanny accuracy — the Ngarrindjeri ancestor who walked to Kangaroo Island before the sea took it (~10–10.7 ka, at seas a hundred feet lower), the Tiwi woman's flood for Bathurst and Melville (8.2–9.65 ka), the Rottnest, Carnac, and Garden Island traditions — analyzed against the regional sea-level curves by Nunn and Reid (*Scientific American* 2015; *Quaternary Science Reviews* 2016): ten of the twenty-one stories preserve relative-dating accuracy across 7,000–11,000 years, implying continuous occupation and transmission chains longer than any other known oral tradition. One family remembers the water coming; the other remembers what the water left behind.

## 12.2 The genetic and linguistic echoes

The genes and tongues of the present are the refugia made audible. Europe: Basque as the small-survival case (six autochthonous maternal lineages; expansion ~4 ka, separation from the pan-European pool ~8 ka; the over-extended Vasconic version demolished in Part Ten); Sardinia as the Neolithic time-capsule — its first inhabitants' maternal lineages dual-sourced (Near-Eastern K1a2d, Western-European U5b1i1), the island's 89 Sardinian-specific haplogroups holding 78.4% of modern Sardinian mtDNA, an X2c2a founder traced to Copper-Age Iberia (8.9±1.7 ka), H3 at its world-frequency peak (18.4%, expansion 9–10.5 ka), Phoenician-era Monte Sirai samples carrying none of the early lines (the first communities stayed genetically isolated even at the colony), and the nuclear record (forty-three Sardinians, 4,100–1,000 BCE) showing a constant ~20% hunter-gatherer fraction, ancient Y R1b-V88 + I2-M223 (Balkan-route markers absent in Anatolian farmers), and modern I2a1a1-M26 at ~39% under extreme drift with male-biased transmission [S — Modi 2017; Olivieri 2017; Matisoo-Smith 2018; Marcus, Posth et al. 2020; Chiang 2018's Gennargentu isolation vault].

The north: the Sámi mitochondrial lines are overwhelmingly western-European in origin (V + U5b1b1, the "Sámi motif" — the post-glacial recolonization remembered in maternal DNA), overlaid by a Siberian (Nganasan-proxy) arrival by 1,610–1,436 BCE at Bolshoy Oleni — the highest Siberian fraction in Europe, carrying the EDAR variant — with Iron-Age Sámi-range extending to southern Ostrobothnia (the Levänluhta lake burials, 300–800 CE): Sámi languages dominated Finland until the first millennium CE, when Finnic-speaking migrants from the north Estonian coast displaced them, documented in parish records into the 1500s [S — Lamnidis et al., *Nature Communications* 2018; Tambets 2004; Ross 2006]. Modern Sámi genetics ≈ 50% steppe-era, 25% Siberian, 15% WHG, 10% farmer — two recolonizations written in one genome. The political layer cuts both ways and should be named: public commentary (Razib Khan, 2025) has inverted the "deepest indigeneity" framing on precisely these data, while a conspiracy-flavored genre claims the opposite suppression; the science supports neither headline — two recolonizations, one documented language shift, and a modern legal status that rests on politics, not ice-age continuity [M — flagged]. The same eastern-Siberian complex anchors the Dene-Yeniseian hypothesis (Part Ten); in the Americas, Na-Dene and Eskimo-Aleut are the living surface of the Syalakh-Belkachi and Paleo-Eskimo layers; in South America, the Toalean foragers' genealogical extinction by the fifth century AD, and in Tierra del Fuego the Selk'nam and Yaghan — canoe nomads of the southern archipelago, the last custodians of the southernmost ice-age refuge, extinguished within a century of European contact (the Beagle's 1826 survey; the Bridges family's missions) — mark the losses. The linguist's framework and its limits are set out in Part Ten; the defensible summary is Nichols' own: language geography preserves refugial geography at exactly the resolution the method allows — spread zones versus accretion zones, and no farther.

## 12.3 The custody wars: bones, consent, and the law

Who owns the ice age? The last thirty years produced the answer in courtrooms and museums, and the answer changed. Three cases anchor the record.

**Kennewick Man / the Ancient One.** Found on 28 July 1996 on the Columbia River's Lake Wallula reservoir shore in Washington (two college students watching a boat race; the coroner called James Chatters, who pronounced the skeleton "Caucasoid" — possibly a Euro-settler — until a spearpoint lodged in the pelvis and radiocarbon returned 8,340–9,200 cal BP). The Army Corps of Engineers seized the bones within weeks and issued a Notice of Intent to Repatriate to five claimant tribes (Umatilla, Nez Perce, Yakama, Colville, Wanapum) — then, by documented decision, dumped roughly a million pounds of rock and fill over the discovery site (alleged at the time, and reported by 60 Minutes and Alan Schneider's team, to forestall complications for salmon, dam, and nuclear-site negotiations). Eight scientists led by Robson Bonnichsen sued; in 2000 Interior Secretary Babbitt found the remains Native American by geography and oral tradition and ordered repatriation; the court blocked; in 2002 Magistrate Jelderks ruled the skeleton Native American but culturally unaffiliated — NAGPRA inapplicable; the 2004 Ninth Circuit upheld: the scientists won the right to study. The 2014 Smithsonian volume (Doug Owsley's team) claimed the closest affinities were Ainu and Polynesian — the morphology's last stand. On 18 June 2015 the genome landed (Rasmussen, Willerslev et al., *Nature* 523:455; a single milligram of metacarpal): he was Native American, his mitochondrial DNA the *root* of haplogroup X2a, his Y chromosome Q-M3, his closest living relatives among the claimant tribes — the Colville, the only tribe to donate comparison DNA, matching at the expected level. The legal endgame ran through the December 2016 WIIN Act rider (signed by Obama), transferring custody via Washington's DAHP; on 18 February 2017, two hundred tribal members reburied him privately at an undisclosed site. Eshe's verdict (Kakaliouras, 2017): the repatriation "symbolically marked the end of a [supposed] non-Indian ancient North America"; Raff's post-mortem noted the press handled the genetics well this time (contrast the Anzick coverage); Kim TallBear's essay — tellingly first published in BuzzFeed — framed the deeper principle — Indigenous data sovereignty. Coda files: the Asatru Folk Assembly's white-pagan claim (rejected); the 2024 damaged-bones report (six broken ribs, an atrophied arm — injuries from life, documented by Owsley's volume); and the pre-Kennewick losses the case almost repeated: the ~10,700-year-old Buhl woman of Idaho and the 8,000-year-old Hourglass Cave man of Colorado, both reburied in the early 1990s without study [A — Owsley's account]. The 2024 NAGPRA final rule (43 CFR 10) then codified the lesson nationwide: museums must obtain Indigenous consent and consultation *before* study — the legal system absorbing in regulation what Kennewick taught in pain.

**Mungo.** Australia held the deepest stakes: the ~40,000-year-old Mungo Lady (found 1968) and Mungo Man (1974; two kilograms of ochre) — the oldest ritual cremation and ochre burial on Earth — came home in 1992 and 2017 (the ANU held Mungo Man for forty years, apologized and relinquished in 2015; the return ran eight hundred kilometers as a black-hearse convoy with hundreds of elders), and on 24 May 2022 both were reburied in a secret location by the traditional owners (Paakantji, Muthi Muthi, Ngiyampaa — the 3TTG), over the Heritage Council of New South Wales's stated non-authorization. The double-key vault concept that was to share custody had dissolved years earlier when the state dismissed the management structures in 2013; the Joulni Keeping Place (Gregory Burgess's design) was never used. The pattern runs from Kow Swamp — the LGM-era robust burial ground (13–9.5 ka; Thorne's "eastern erectus" claims long dead; Brothwell's artificial-deformation reading of KS5; reburied to the Yorta Yorta at Echuca in 1990, surviving now only in casts at ANU and the Natural History Museum) — to the Willandra trackways (the largest ice-age footprint assemblage, on the last margin of a dying lake system) to the 3,177-hectare gazettal of 2022 and the National Heritage listing fight of 2025 (a local MP's "pointless travesty" statement on the record). The science increasingly concedes the point: some questions are not ours to answer.

**The governance frontier.** The contrast completing the file: the Ayta Denisovan study, whose genomic data sit behind an Indigenous-governed access committee (EGA EGAS00001005408; the Swedish Research Council and Wallenberg funding came with the governance attached), against the open-download model of the great repositories — and the Allen Ancient DNA Resource, whose eight-year grant expired in September 2025 with its NIH renewal scored "excellent" but likely dead in the federal funding contraction [S — Harvard Crimson, 2025-10-23; 67,000+ downloads to date; the field's central infrastructure at risk as of this writing, with the rescue question — Wellcome, ENA mirrors, Sloan — live]. The future of the dead is a data-policy question too.

## 12.4 The concept comes home: climate refugia now

The ice age's vocabulary has returned — this time with us holding the pen. Conservation science now maps **climate refugia** formally: places where species persist through climate change, managed deliberately as "slow lanes" buying time (Keppel, Stralberg, Morelli & Bátori, *Trends in Ecology & Evolution* 2024 — the current management bible; Morelli's Northeast CASC program frames refugia as the hedge against extinction's clock); validated bottom-up with empirical protocols (Barrows 2020); and corrected by the critical refinement that an Anthropocene refugium must be ecologically suitable *and* under low human pressure, since historical range contractions have already biased our baselines — Monsarrat et al.'s bison-and-tiger case study (*Philosophical Transactions* 2019, cited 99) shows today's protected "refugia" are often just the least-bad fragments of shrunken ranges, and Faurby & Araújo (2018) quantify how anthropogenic contractions bias the climate forecasts built on them. The microrefugia finding — shaded valleys, fog belts, slope aspects letting species persist where regional models say they cannot (Serra-Diaz's disturbance-interaction models; Lenoir's mountain transects) — is the modern echo of the cryptic-refugia war (Part One); and the field data now show migration running slower on land, where human fragmentation blocks it, than at sea.

The parallels with this document are not decorative. The ice-age refuge had peninsulas, mountains, drowned shelves, cryptic northern pockets, and networks between refuges that mattered as much as the refuges themselves (Peña Capón's 700 km of alliance through the coldest millennium). Twenty-first-century conservation rediscovers each element: assisted migration as recolonization, corridors as the safety net (Carroll's corridor work: the connections between refugia matter as much as the refuges themselves), genetic rescue as the deliberate version of the gene flow that saved the Wrangel mammoths' genome from meltdown — and the founder-effect arithmetic of Finnish disease heritage (Part Eight) as the warning about bottlenecks deliberately created. The ice age's last lesson is addressed to its sole surviving student: *you have been the refugee; now you are the coastline everyone else is betting on.*

## 13.1 The founder and the labs

The paleogenomics era begins with Svante Pääbo (Part Eight): mummy DNA in 1984 (retracted as contamination), the first PCR on ancient remains in 1988 with Allan Wilson, the 1997 Neanderthal mitochondrion with Mark Stoneking's blind replication, the Max Planck Institute for Evolutionary Anthropology (founded 1997, Pääbo one of its five founding directors), the 2010 Neanderthal draft and Denisovan discovery, Meyer's single-stranded genome, "Denny," sediment DNA, and the Nobel Prize of October 2022. The ecosystem he spawned now runs on five great centers and their satellites [S — Callaway's *Nature* profile; the labs' own publication lists]: **Leipzig** (Pääbo; Johannes Krause, now director at the Max Planck Institute for Chemical Ecology in Jena; Kay Prüfer; the 10,016-ancient-genome selection megastudy with Akbari and Patrick Perry, *Nature* 654:419, 2026 — seven years of collection-building with 250-plus collaborating archaeologists, the Broad's press release notes); **Harvard** (David Reich's lab — the Allen Ancient DNA Resource, the 1,000-ancient-Europeans study, the Dzudzuana preprint that never published, the 2018 "No evidence for unknown archaic ancestry in South Asia" salvo and the Moorjani lab's 2025 India flagship answering it fifty thousand years deep); **Copenhagen** (Eske Willerslev — GeoGenetics, the Lundbeck-funded 5,000-ancient-genomes program, the Malaspinas Sahul paper, the Kennewick genome); **Uppsala–Tübingen–Stockholm** (Mattias Jakobsson's Ayta work with the Indigenous-governed data access; Cosimo Posth's European rewrites; Love Dalén's Centre for Palaeogenetics and the Wrangel mammoth genomics); and the **China–Frankfurt axis** (Qiaomei Fu at the IVPP; the Tianyuan/AR-series record; He Yu's Amur work). The forensic-dating wing — Katerina Douka and Tom Higham's Oxford laboratory — underwrites the Sahul and European chronologies; the isotope wing (Pederzani's Ranis work) underwrites the cold-endurance claims; and the Australian wing (the Willerslev group's deep Aboriginal collaborations, repatriation-framed) holds the deepest continuous record on Earth.

The refugium-theory founders: Geoffrey Hewitt (East Anglia) and John Avise (phylogeography's coiner). The African school: Henshilwood and d'Errico at Blombos, Stanley Ambrose at Enkapune ya Muto, Dani Nadel at Ohalo. The reader will notice what this roster shares with Part Ten's referee list: it is the same community, wearing its two hats.

## 13.3 The money

The field runs on six visible streams, and the grant numbers themselves are a map [S — public records]: **US federal grants** — NIH (notably HG012287 for the AADR) and NSF — now under historic stress; **private tech-philanthropy** — Paul Allen's foundation, which funded the Ancient DNA Resource until its eight-year grant ended in September 2025; the John Templeton Foundation's $15.5M "Ancient DNA Atlas of Humanity" (ten thousand ancient individuals across fifty thousand years, five times the published genome count, with ethics meetings earmarked — the Kennewick lesson priced into a grant); the Lundbeck Foundation's DKK 60 million Copenhagen program, justified on brain-disorder genetics and delivering, among much else, the oldest genome then sequenced at ~34,000 years; the Knut & Alice Wallenberg grants behind the Ayta work; HHMI; **the European Research Council** — 805478 (MULTIPALEOIBERIA, the Peña Capón exchange work), 647787 (the Sungir genomes), 885729 (a thousand ancient *animal* genomes: the livestock mirror of the human projects); **the German MPG/DFG system**; **UK Wellcome** (Pontus Skoglund's £1.7M thousand-ancient-British-genomes program, tied to UK Biobank's medical frame); and **national programs** — Norway's SapienCE centre of excellence (which funded the Shiyu work in China), Japan, China's NSFC, South Africa's NRF and its corporate partners (the Anglo American Chairman's Fund has supported Blombos), and Australia's ARC. Two structural observations: the great repositories double as infrastructure and as single points of failure (the AADR's stranded "excellent"-scored NIH renewal, 67,000+ downloads to date, the rescue question — Wellcome, ENA mirrors, Sloan — open as of this writing); and the money increasingly arrives with governance strings attached — the Ayta access committee, the Lundbeck rationale, Templeton's ethics earmarks — the funding world absorbing the Kennewick lesson at its own pace.

## 13.4 The referee class, and the rules they enforce

The document's controversies (Part Ten) share a hidden author: the professional skeptic. Stuart Fiedel across Paisley, Cooper's Ferry, and the Delmarva claims; James Chatters against Chiquihuite (and against the Solutrean hypothesis before it); Todd Surovell against the impact hypothesis (2009) and Monte Verde (2026); Chris Stringer against the 930 ka bottleneck; the Cobbold-1000 signatories against ancient-DNA over-reach; Robert Bednarik against art-history exceptionalism; Chronis Tzedakis against cryptic refugia; the Sauvet group against the U-Th dates, armed with the caves' own tables; David Reich's lab against the West African ghost (2018) — with the 2026 TRACE result splitting the difference. The field's unwritten rules, enforceable only by reputation: **publish your failure modes** (Pike's caveats about floor charcoal; Prüfer's cattle glue; Koller's phthalates; the FitCoal team's own stated assumptions); **replicate across labs before you announce** (the Krings–Stoneking protocol of 1997 still defines credibility); and **never let a permit monopoly, a museum label, or a headline outlive its evidence** (Monte Verde's fifty-year exclusivity now evidence in the case against it; Divje babe's label; Ryan–Pitman's one-day acceptance). The reader holding this document has now seen every one of those rules broken and enforced at least once — which is, in the end, why the claims worth keeping have survived.

# APPENDIX B — GLOSSARY

- **aDNA**: ancient DNA, recovered from bones, teeth, and sediment.
- **ANE / ANS**: Ancient North Eurasians (Yana→Mal'ta→Afontova line) / Ancient North Siberians (the Yana population).
- **ARG**: ancestral recombination graph; a complete map of genomic ancestry used by the TRACE method.
- **Bottleneck**: a severe, temporary reduction in population size, leaving reduced genetic diversity.
- **Cryptic refugium**: a refuge north of the conventional glacial limits, inferred for plants and small animals; unproven for humans.
- **D-O events**: Dansgaard–Oeschger events — abrupt Greenland warmings during glacials, ~25 in the last glacial, paced near 1,470 years.
- **Epi-Gravettian**: the post-Gravettian technocomplex of Italy and the Balkans (also Epigravettian), made by the Balkan-refuge lineages.
- **FADS1**: a gene region associated with fat metabolism; its ancestral allele marks northern fat-based diets.
- **Founder effect**: the genetic profile of a population descended from a small number of ancestors.
- **Ghost population**: a population inferred genetically but never sampled as remains.
- **Haplogroup**: a branch of the human mitochondrial (maternal) or Y-chromosomal (paternal) family tree.
- **Heinrich event**: an iceberg armada from collapsing ice sheets, marking the coldest pre-warming phase.
- **IUP**: Initial Upper Palaeolithic — the first modern-human technocomplex in Eurasia, ~45 ka.
- **LGM**: Last Glacial Maximum, ~26.5–19 ka; the deepest freeze of the last glacial.
- **MIS**: Marine Isotope Stage; odd = warm, even = glacial.
- **MWP**: Meltwater Pulse; MWP-1A (14.65–14.31 ka) is the largest.
- **Ne (effective population size)**: the size of a genetically idealized population with the same diversity as the real one — always smaller than the census.
- **Pedigree tag**: this document's confidence labels — [S] strong, [P] preprint, [C] contested, [M] media/unverified.
- **Phylogeography**: the study of how genealogical lineages are distributed geographically; the source of refugium theory.
- **ROH (runs of homozygosity)**: long identical chromosome stretches indicating recent inbreeding or small population size.
- **sedaDNA**: sedimentary ancient DNA — genetic material recovered directly from cave or lake deposits.
- **SPD**: summed probability distribution of radiocarbon dates; an archaeological-demographic proxy.
- **Standstill**: the hypothesized isolation of the founding Native American population in or near Beringia.
- **U-Th dating**: uranium-thorium radiometric dating of calcite crusts; the method at the center of the cave-art chronology war.
- **Varve**: an annually deposited sediment layer (summer aragonite over winter detritus); the Levant's lake record reads like a yearly climate diary.
- **WHG / EHG / CHG**: Western / Eastern / Caucasus hunter-gatherer genetic components, the post-LGM building blocks of Europe.
